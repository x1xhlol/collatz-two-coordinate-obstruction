\begin{abstract}
We prove two obstructions to matrix-interpretation proofs for the
eleven-rule Collatz system of Yolcu, Aaronson, and Heule.
For nonnegative real affine maps in two coordinates, if every symbol
matrix has first diagonal entry at least one, weak orientation of all
eleven rules forces every first-coordinate offset gap to vanish.
This holds separately for the original and word-reversed systems,
with arbitrary admissible boundary maps and no coefficient bound.
For the reversed system we also remove every diagonal lower bound:
nonnegativity and the eleven weak comparisons alone force equality of
the offset vectors in both rules eligible for the reversed TOP step,
$da\to d$ and $db\to dg$. Thus neither rule can have a positive
uniform decrement in this two-coordinate real affine format.
The proofs include singular matrices and zero entries. We give the
matrix and scalar classifications, the resonance calculation, and the
growth argument underlying the unrestricted reversed result.
For the forward system in arbitrary finite dimension, we also prove
a five-stage growth bound for the three root gaps and show that a
positive contracting left row for the binary matrix makes them vanish.
Separately, we prove that every finite set of nonnegative offsets has
a dyadic progression of positive translating integers for which all
trajectories coalesce at one common time with equal odd counts. Specializing the
common endpoint to a power of two gives arbitrarily large translates
with equal finite first hitting times of one, including consecutive
runs of every prescribed length. Lean 4 checks these statements and
their supporting algebra.
The unrestricted forward TOP problem, unrestricted higher dimensions,
and the Collatz conjecture remain unresolved by these results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Yolcu, Aaronson, and Heule constructed a mixed binary--ternary string
rewriting system whose termination is equivalent to the Collatz
conjecture~\cite[Theorem~3.17]{yah}. Their construction turns a question
about integer trajectories into a termination problem for eleven rules.
The system, its arithmetic interpretation, and that equivalence are
their results. This paper studies restrictions on possible
matrix-interpretation proofs for their system and a separate
synchronization property of finite patterns of Collatz starting values.

Rule removal builds a termination proof in stages. An interpretation
orients all remaining rules weakly and a nonempty subset strictly;
relative termination then permits removal of that subset. Repeating
the process can establish termination~\cite[Theorems~2.6 and~2.15]{yah}.
The first step therefore needs at least one strict rule while retaining
weak comparisons for the complete system.

Our first result rules out that first step in two coordinates for the
strictly monotone affine format defined below. It quantifies over all
admissible nonnegative real coefficients, including irrational ones,
and imposes no upper bound. A failed bounded SAT search cannot establish
this conclusion: it leaves larger coefficients and coefficients outside
its encoding unexamined. The obstruction is algebraic. It forces all
eleven candidate strict gaps to vanish, rather than excluding one
chosen rule or one coefficient range.

Dimension restrictions matter in this setting. Yolcu, Aaronson, and
Heule report that, for some weakened variants, their tool found only
interpretations of dimension five~\cite[Section~1]{yah}. Their Table~1 also
records a termination proof using five-dimensional natural matrix
interpretations for the subsystem omitting \(bf\to ga\) in our notation.
These are search outcomes, not proofs
of minimal dimension. The present result supplies a coefficient-independent
exclusion for the full system in dimension two, within a narrower,
precisely stated interpretation format.

The proof has two parts. First, the matrix comparisons force the five
digit matrices to share a triangular orientation, with unit first
diagonals. Second, an affine analysis forces every first-offset gap to
zero. The upper triangular case requires retaining products between
matrix entries and offsets, including several zero-slope cases.
A second result removes the diagonal bounds for the reversed
\textsc{top} problem. Assuming a strict eligible rule, it first forces
a common triangular orientation. It then excludes each possible
active diagonal, using a comparison between binary-prefix growth and
ternary-word bounds in the expanding case.
Section~\ref{sec:further-necessary} treats a complementary forward
case without a dimension bound. Every five stages of an explicit
conversion use each eligible root rule, yielding a quantitative growth
bound. A positive contracting left row for the binary matrix $A$
makes all three root gaps vanish. This contraction condition is
incompatible with the first-diagonal lower bound in the first theorem;
it imposes no contraction condition on the other digit matrices.
Section~\ref{sec:finite-patterns} gives a direct arithmetic result.
For every finite set $F\subseteq\mathbb N_0$ and every lower bound,
there is a larger integer $x$ such that all $x+f$, $f\in F$, first
reach one at the same finite time, with the same number of odd steps.
The proof synchronizes affine families on a common dyadic progression
and then chooses a power-of-two endpoint. In particular, arbitrarily
long consecutive runs with this property exist. The translating
integer is chosen by the construction; convergence from an arbitrary
prescribed starting value is not a conclusion.
All these results are formalized in Lean.
Appendix~\ref{app:lean-statements} reproduces the definitions and
exact principal theorem types.

\subsection{Related work}
Matrix-interpretation obstructions for Collatz rewriting already appear
in Yolcu, Aaronson, and Heule's paper. Their starting point is Zantema's
seven-rule unary system, whose termination is equivalent to the Collatz
conjecture~\cite[Section~10, Theorem~16]{zantema}. For that system,
denoted $Z$ in~\cite{yah}, Theorem~3.8 of~\cite{yah} excludes natural
matrix interpretations in every finite dimension that weakly orient all
rules and strictly orient at least one, under extended monotonicity.
Thus no direct rule-removal step is possible in that format.

Their dependency-pair result is also an impossibility theorem. After
other pairs have been removed, Lemma~3.9 reduces the problem to two
residual pairs. Theorem~3.10 excludes a natural matrix interpretation,
in any dimension, that strictly orients either residual pair while
weakly orienting $Z$, even with only weak monotonicity. Both obstructions
also hold for the reversed unary system~\cite[Section~3.1.1]{yah}.
The dependency-pair statement therefore allows the earlier removals;
it identifies the obstruction that remains afterward.

Our results address the eleven-rule mixed binary--ternary system
instead. Both quantify over nonnegative real coefficients, including
irrational ones, in dimension two. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} retains the
diagonal bound in~\eqref{eq:admissible} and makes all eleven
first-offset gaps zero in either word orientation.

Theorem~\ref{thm:reversed-top} removes every diagonal lower bound for the reversed
system. Under its eleven weak comparisons, the offset vectors of
$da$ and $d$ coincide, as do those of $db$ and $dg$. Consequently
neither of these two rules admits a uniform positive decrement in
either output coordinate. These are exactly the eligible reversed
dynamic rules in the relative top-removal reduction of
Yolcu--Aaronson--Heule~\cite[Lemma~3.18(2)]{yah}. Their interpretation
method requires only weak monotonicity in this setting
\cite[Theorem~2.15]{yah}.

The unary and mixed-base results therefore have different scopes:
neither is a direct strengthening of the other. The new reversed
result concerns the two eligible rules, not all eleven offsets. It
does not exclude the forward boundary-rule \textsc{top} route, marked
dependency-pair transformations of the mixed-base system, higher
dimensions, or arctic interpretations.

The proof mechanisms also differ. The unary argument represents a
configuration value by an $\mathbb N$-rational sequence $u^TM^nv$ and
contradicts its eventual growth properties
\cite[Theorem~3.5, Corollary~3.6, and Theorems~3.7--3.8]{yah}.
Our proof uses the mixed-base swap inequalities to force a common
triangular orientation, then eliminates the affine gaps. The unrestricted
reversed argument also compares growth along binary prefixes with
bounds for ternary representations of the same integers. The relevant
rule-removal framework and the distinction between extended and weak
monotonicity are due to Endrullis, Waldmann, and
Zantema~\cite[Theorems~2--3]{ewz}. Their matrix criterion separates the
coefficient comparisons from strictness of the first offset
\cite[Lemma~4]{ewz}; Section~\ref{sec:format} gives the fixed-decrement
real version used here. The contributions are the two precise
coefficient-independent obstructions for the mixed-base system,
the forward growth and contraction result in
Section~\ref{sec:further-necessary}, and their Lean proofs. The forward
result uses only the six digit swaps and three root comparisons,
omitting both dynamic-boundary rules. The separate arithmetic construction
and formalization in Section~\ref{sec:finite-patterns} are discussed
alongside earlier work on consecutive Collatz starting values there.


\section{The system and the interpretation format}
\label{sec:format}
We use the alphabet $\{a,b,c,d,e,f,g\}$ and the ASCII names from
the authors' implementation~\cite{yahcode}. Table~\ref{tab:symbols}
relates these names to the symbols in their article. The rules are
their full mixed-base system~\cite[Section~3.2]{yah},
with the leftmost symbol interpreted as the outermost map:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rules}
\begin{array}{lll}
 ad\to d, & bd\to gd, & \\
 ae\to ea, & af\to eb, & ag\to fa,\\
 be\to fb, & bf\to ga, & bg\to gb,\\
 ce\to cb, & cf\to caa, & cg\to cab.
\end{array}
\end{equation}

The five symbols $a,b,e,f,g$ are called digits; $c,d$ are boundary symbols.
Assign to each symbol $s$ an affine map $F_s(x)=M_sx+v_s$ on $\R^2$.
An interpretation is \emph{admissible} when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:admissible}
M_s\in\nn^{2\times2},\qquad v_s\in\nn^2,\qquad (M_s)_{00}\ge1
\quad\text{for every }s.
\end{equation}
For words, composition determines $M_w,v_w$; in particular,
$M_{uv}=M_uM_v$ and $v_{uv}=M_uv_v+v_u$.
A rule $\ell\to r$ is \emph{weak} if $M_\ell\ge M_r$ and $v_\ell\ge v_r$
entrywise. Its first-offset gap is
$\gap(\ell,r)=(v_\ell)_0-(v_r)_0$.
The strictness criterion considered here is $\gap(\ell,r)>0$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For every admissible interpretation in two real coordinates, weak
orientation of all eleven rules in \eqref{eq:rules} implies
$\gap(\ell,r)=0$ for every rule. The same assertion holds for the eleven
rules $\rev(\ell)\to\rev(r)$, where each word is reversed and the direction
of every rule is retained.
\end{theorem}

The two orientations are separate implications, not simultaneous
hypotheses. There is no coefficient bound, integrality requirement,
nonsingularity assumption, or prescribed boundary shape. The conclusion
concerns the first offsets; it does not assert equality of the complete
affine maps. Sections 3--6 give the matrix classifications and affine case analyses
that prove this theorem. The reversed system admits a stronger result
for its two dynamic rules.

\begin{theorem}[Reversed TOP obstruction]\label{thm:reversed-top}
Let the seven symbol interpretations be nonnegative real affine maps
on $\R^2$, with no diagonal lower bound. If all eleven word-reversed
rules are weakly oriented coefficientwise, then
\[
v_{da}=v_d,\qquad v_{db}=v_{dg}.
\]
These are equalities of the entire offset vectors. In particular,
neither $da\to d$ nor $db\to dg$ has a positive offset gap in either
coordinate.
\end{theorem}

Section~\ref{sec:reversed-top} proves this result. It does not claim
that the other nine reversed gaps vanish without the diagonal bounds.
For example, take $a,b$ to be the identity, $c,d$ the constant zero map,
and $e,f,g$ the same positive translation. All reversed rules are weak,
but the three rules with inner symbol $c$ have positive offset gaps.

\subsection{Scope and soundness}
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} concerns the strictly monotone, full-context
format in \eqref{eq:admissible}. Every symbol, including both
boundaries, must satisfy $(M_s)_{00}\ge1$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:reversed-top} instead concerns the two rules eligible
for the reversed relative top-removal step in
Yolcu--Aaronson--Heule's Lemma~3.18(2)~\cite{yah}. At the root,
strict decrease need not be preserved by every surrounding symbol,
so only weak monotonicity is required.
The result excludes this first reversed \textsc{top} step using two
nonnegative real affine coordinates and the strictness criterion below.
It does not resolve the forward \textsc{top} problem, arctic
interpretations, other strict orders, or higher dimensions.

Real coefficients require a fixed positive decrement. For $\delta>0$,
put
\[
x\succ_\delta y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x_0\ge y_0+\delta\ \text{ and }\ x_1\ge y_1,
\qquad x,y\in\nn^2.
\]
Along a decrease, $\lfloor x_0/\delta\rfloor$ strictly decreases in
$\mathbb N$, so $\succ_\delta$ is well-founded. Nonnegative affine maps
preserve coordinatewise weak order. The bound $(M_s)_{00}\ge1$ also
preserves $\succ_\delta$, since
\[
\bigl(F_s(x)-F_s(y)\bigr)_0
 = (M_s)_{00}(x_0-y_0)+(M_s)_{01}(x_1-y_1)\ge\delta.
\]
A weak rule with first-offset gap $\varepsilon>0$ decreases by at least
$\varepsilon$ at every nonnegative input. For a finite nonempty set of
strict rules, the least such gap supplies a common $\delta>0$.
These observations explain the real-coefficient certificate format;
ordinary strict inequality on the reals alone would not suffice.
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} prevents even this first choice of a strict rule.
The preservation, evaluation, and well-foundedness claims above are
formalized separately in \code{FullTwoSoundness.lean}.
For the root comparisons of Theorem~\ref{thm:reversed-top}, a positive
uniform decrement at every nonnegative input would in particular hold
at zero. The offset equalities rule this out without any assumption
that strict decrease is preserved inside a context.

The weak comparisons and uniform strict decrements in this format are
required on the entire nonnegative orthant, including zero. A method
that requires decrease only on inputs reachable from represented
configurations lies outside these obstructions. In particular, a zero
offset gap does not exclude positive decrease on such a restricted set.

\section{Reduction of the digit matrices}
\label{sec:matrix-reduction}
Write $A,B,C,D,E,F,G$ for the seven matrices. Throughout this section,
all seven matrices are entrywise nonnegative and have $(0,0)$ entry at
least one. We first treat the forward orientation. Its six digit
comparisons are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:swaps}
AE\ge EA,\quad AF\ge EB,\quad AG\ge FA,\quad
BE\ge FB,\quad BF\ge GA,\quad BG\ge GB.
\end{equation}
The five boundary comparisons are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-boundaries}
AD\ge D,\qquad BD\ge GD,\qquad
CE\ge CB,\qquad CF\ge CA^2,\qquad CG\ge CAB.
\end{equation}
All inequalities in this section are entrywise. In particular, they may
be multiplied on either side by a nonnegative matrix. None of the
arguments below uses an affine offset.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:matrix}
Suppose seven nonnegative real $2\times2$ matrices have $(0,0)$ entries
at least one and satisfy \eqref{eq:swaps} and
\eqref{eq:matrix-boundaries}. Then the five digit matrices
$A,B,E,F,G$ are all upper triangular or all lower triangular in the
original coordinates, and
\[
A_{00}=B_{00}=E_{00}=F_{00}=G_{00}=1.
\]
The same conclusion holds for the matrix comparisons of the reversed
rules.
\end{lemma}

The distinction between triangularity in the original coordinates and
triangularity after a change of basis matters: the order and the
distinguished coordinate in \eqref{eq:admissible} are fixed. We prove
the lemma by excluding the case in which the sum of the digit matrices
has both off-diagonal entries positive. The required algebraic
classifications, including the singular cases, are given below.

\subsection{From weak swaps to exact swaps}
Put $P=A+B$ and $H=A+B+E+F+G$. The sum of the six differences in
\eqref{eq:swaps} is
\[
K=PH-HP\ge0.
\]
Since $\tr K=0$, both diagonal entries of $K$ vanish. Cyclicity of the
trace also gives $\tr(HK)=0$. Consequently
\[
H_{01}K_{10}+H_{10}K_{01}=0.
\]
If $H_{01},H_{10}>0$, this forces $K_{01}=K_{10}=0$. Each of the six
nonnegative differences that sum to $K$ must then be zero. We call the
resulting identities the \emph{exact swaps}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exact-swaps}
AE=EA,\quad AF=EB,\quad AG=FA,\quad
BE=FB,\quad BF=GA,\quad BG=GB.
\end{equation}
Even without the strict inequalities on $H$, the same trace argument
shows that every diagonal entry of every swap difference is zero. We
will use this weaker conclusion after proving triangularity.

We use the following elementary identities for real $2\times2$
matrices:
\begin{align}
X^2&=(\tr X)X-(\det X)I,\label{eq:matrix-ch}\\
RXR&=\tr(RX)R+(\det R)X-(\det R)(\tr X)I,
  \label{eq:matrix-sandwich}\\
XY+YX&=(\tr X)Y+(\tr Y)X+
 \bigl(\tr(XY)-\tr X\,\tr Y\bigr)I.
  \label{eq:matrix-polarized}
\end{align}
The first is Cayley--Hamilton; the other two follow by expanding the
four entries. Thus, if $\det R=0$ and $\beta=\tr R\ne0$, then
$R^2=\beta R$ and $RXR=\tr(RX)R$. These rank-one identities account for
the singular branches of the proof.

\subsection{The boundary comparison for a common rank-one action}
The same scalar comparison will be used twice.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:matrix-rankone-boundary}
Assume the standing nonnegativity and first-diagonal hypotheses and
\eqref{eq:matrix-boundaries}. Let $R\ge0$ satisfy $R_{00}>0$ and, for
real numbers $\alpha,\beta$,
\[
RA=RB=AR=\alpha R,\qquad RG=FR=\beta R.
\]
Then $\alpha=\beta=1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The $(0,0)$ entry of $RA=\alpha R$ gives $\alpha\ge A_{00}\ge1$.
Also $(RD)_{00}>0$ and $(CR)_{00}>0$, because $C_{00},D_{00}\ge1$.
Multiplying $BD\ge GD$ on the left by $R$ gives
$\alpha RD\ge\beta RD$, hence $\alpha\ge\beta$. Multiplying
$CF\ge CA^2$ on the right by $R$ gives
$\beta CR\ge\alpha^2 CR$, hence $\beta\ge\alpha^2$. Therefore
\[
1\le\alpha,\qquad \alpha^2\le\beta\le\alpha,
\]
which implies the assertion.
\end{proof}

We also need a specifically two-dimensional observation about stationary
vectors. It supplies a right action from the left action arising in the
singular-middle case.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:matrix-stationary}
Let $A,B\ge0$ be $2\times2$ matrices with $A_{00},B_{00}>0$. Suppose
$w$ is a strictly positive row, $\alpha>0$,
$wA=wB=\alpha w$, and a column $u$ satisfies
\[
ABu=BAu=\alpha^2u.
\]
Then $Au=Bu=\alpha u$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Both matrices preserve the one-dimensional space $\ker w$. Their
actions on that space are multiplication by
\[
\kappa_A=\tr A-\alpha,\qquad
\kappa_B=\tr B-\alpha.
\]
The positive left eigenrow and nonnegativity give
$A_{ii},B_{ii}\le\alpha$; the positive $(0,0)$ entries give
\[
-\alpha<\kappa_A,\kappa_B\le\alpha.
\]
Set $x=(A-\alpha I)u$ and $y=(B-\alpha I)u$. These vectors belong to
$\ker w$, and the two product equations become
\[
\alpha x+\kappa_Ay=0,\qquad
\alpha y+\kappa_Bx=0.
\]
If $\kappa_A<\alpha$, the displayed bounds imply
$\kappa_A\kappa_B<\alpha^2$, so $x=y=0$. If
$\kappa_A=\alpha$, both diagonal entries of $A$ equal $\alpha$;
$wA=\alpha w$ then makes both off-diagonal entries zero. Thus
$A=\alpha I$, and $ABu=\alpha^2u$ gives the same conclusion.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A singular middle digit}
Here is the precise singular classification needed for the proof.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:matrix-singular-middle}
Let $A,B,E,F,G\ge0$ satisfy the exact swaps and have strictly positive
$(0,0)$ entries. If $\det F=0$, then, with $\beta=\tr F>0$, there is
$\alpha>0$ such that
\begin{gather}
AB=BA,\qquad FA=FB=\alpha F,\qquad FE=F^2=FG=\beta F,
 \label{eq:singular-middle-left}\\
ABF=BAF=\alpha^2F.\label{eq:singular-middle-products}
\end{gather}
If in addition $H_{01},H_{10}>0$, then $F_{01},F_{10}>0$ and
$AF=BF=\alpha F$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The exact swaps give
\[
ABF=FA^2,\qquad BAF=FB^2.
\]
Using $F^2=\beta F$ in the first identity gives
$FA^2F=\beta FA^2$. Cayley--Hamilton for $A$, whose trace is positive,
reduces this equation to $FAF=\beta FA$. The rank-one sandwich identity
therefore gives $FA=aF$, where $a=\tr(FA)/\beta$.
Similarly $FB=bF$. Their $(0,0)$ entries show $a,b>0$.

Let $L=AB-BA$. Then $FL=0$ and $\tr L=0$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:matrix-polarized} applied to $F,L$ gives
$LF=\beta L$. On the other hand, the two product identities give
$LF=(a^2-b^2)F$. Taking traces yields $a^2=b^2$, hence $a=b=\alpha$,
and then $L=0$. Multiplying $BE=FB$ and $AG=FA$ on the left by $F$
now gives $FE=FG=\beta F$. This proves
\eqref{eq:singular-middle-left}--\eqref{eq:singular-middle-products}.

These actions imply $FH=(2\alpha+3\beta)F$. If $F_{01}=0$, its
$(0,1)$ entry would give $F_{00}H_{01}=0$. Thus the row
$w=(F_{00},F_{01})$ is strictly positive. Apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:matrix-stationary} to each column of $F$ to obtain
$AF=BF=\alpha F$.

To see that $F_{10}>0$, suppose instead that $F_{10}=0$.
The determinant condition and $F_{00}>0$ give $F_{11}=0$.
The $(1,0)$ entries of $AG=FA$ and $BE=FB$, together with
$G_{00},E_{00}>0$, then force $A_{10}=B_{10}=0$.
The same entries of $AF=EB$ and $BF=GA$ force
$E_{10}=G_{10}=0$. This contradicts $H_{10}>0$.
\end{proof}

Return to the standing first-diagonal bounds. Apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:matrix-rankone-boundary} with $R=F$ to obtain
$\alpha=\beta=1$. But $\tr F=1$ and $F_{00}\ge1$ imply $F_{11}=0$;
then
\[
0=\det F=-F_{01}F_{10}<0.
\]
This excludes a singular $F$ whenever both off-diagonals of $H$ are
positive.

\subsection{Singular binary digits with an invertible middle digit}
Assume next that $\det F\ne0$. Taking determinants in $AF=EB$ and
$BE=FB$ gives $\det A=\det B$. If this common determinant is nonzero,
$AG=FA$ and $BE=FB$ also give
$\det E=\det F=\det G\ne0$. The remaining singular branch is therefore
exactly $\det A=\det B=0$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:matrix-singular-binary}
Let real $2\times2$ matrices satisfy the exact swaps, with
\[
\det A=\det B=0,\qquad \det F\ne0,\qquad
\tr A,\tr B,\tr F>0.
\]
If $A\ne B$, there are $\alpha,\beta>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:singular-binary-shape}
\begin{gathered}
\tr A=\tr B=\alpha,\qquad
A^2=AB=\alpha A,\qquad BA=B^2=\alpha B,\\
F=\beta I,\qquad E=(\beta/\alpha)A,
\qquad G=(\beta/\alpha)B.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
If instead $A=B\ge0$, $E\ge0$, and $A_{00},E_{00}>0$, there is
$\beta>0$ such that, for $\alpha=\tr A>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:equal-singular-actions}
A^2=\alpha A,\qquad
AE=EA=AF=FA=AG=GA=\beta A.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $F$ is invertible, $ABF=FA^2$ and $BAF=FB^2$ imply
\[
\tr(AB)=(\tr A)^2=(\tr B)^2.
\]
The positive traces are equal; call their value $\alpha$.
For $N=B-A$, the two-dimensional identity
\[
\det(B-A)=\det A+\det B+\tr(AB)-\tr A\,\tr B
\]
gives $\tr N=\det N=\tr(AN)=0$. If $A\ne B$, then
$N\ne0$ and $N^2=0$. Its image and kernel are the same line.
The sandwich identity gives $NAN=0$, so $A$ preserves this line.
Consequently
\[
AN=aN,\qquad NA=(\alpha-a)N
\]
for some $a$. The second identity follows from
\eqref{eq:matrix-polarized}; $A^2=\alpha A$ makes $a=0$ or
$a=\alpha$.

The value $a=\alpha$ would give $AB=\alpha B$ and $BA=\alpha A$.
The two intertwining identities with $F$ would then yield
$BF=FA$ and $AF=FB$, hence $FN+NF=0$.
Taking traces gives $\tr(FN)=0$, and
\eqref{eq:matrix-polarized} reduces to $(\tr F)N=0$, a contradiction.
Thus $a=0$, proving $AB=\alpha A$ and $BA=\alpha B$.
The same intertwining identities now say that $F$ commutes with both
$A$ and $B$.

A nonscalar $2\times2$ matrix has centralizer $\{xI+yA:x,y\in\R\}$.
To see this, choose a column $v$ for which $v,Av$ are independent.
A commuting matrix is determined by its value $xv+yAv$ on $v$, since
its value on $Av$ must then be $A(xv+yAv)$.
Here $A$ is nonscalar because it is singular with positive trace.
Writing $F=xI+yA$, commutation with $B$ gives
$y\alpha(A-B)=0$, so $F=\beta I$; its positive trace gives $\beta>0$.
Write $E=x'I+y'A$ using $AE=EA$. Then $AF=EB$ gives
\[
\beta A=x'B+y'\alpha A.
\]
The distinct matrices $A,B$ with the same nonzero trace are linearly
independent, so $x'=0$ and $y'=\beta/\alpha$.
The corresponding argument with $BG=GB$ and $BF=GA$ gives $G$.

For $A=B$, use $AE=EA$, $A^2=\alpha A$, and
$AEA=\tr(AE)A$. These give $AE=EA=\beta A$, where
$\beta=\tr(AE)/\alpha>0$; positivity follows from the $(0,0)$ entry.
The remaining exact swaps give all the other identities in
\eqref{eq:equal-singular-actions}.
\end{proof}

We now exclude both alternatives under the standing boundary and
first-diagonal hypotheses. If $A=B$, use $R=A$ in
Lemma~\ref{lem:matrix-rankone-boundary}. It gives
$\alpha=\beta=1$. Equation~\eqref{eq:equal-singular-actions} also gives
\[
AH=HA=(2\alpha+3\beta)A.
\]
The $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ entries force $A_{01},A_{10}>0$ when
$H_{01},H_{10}>0$. This contradicts
$\tr A=1$, $A_{00}\ge1$, and $\det A=0$.

If $A\ne B$, substitute \eqref{eq:singular-binary-shape} in $BD\ge GD$.
Since $(BD)_{00}>0$, it gives $\beta\le\alpha$.
Multiplying $CF\ge CA^2$ on the right by $A$ and using
$(CA)_{00}>0$ gives $\beta\ge\alpha^2$.
Also $\alpha=\tr A\ge1$, so again $\alpha=\beta=1$.
The first-diagonal bounds now force
\[
A_{00}=B_{00}=1,\qquad A_{11}=B_{11}=0,
\qquad E=A,\quad F=I,\quad G=B.
\]
The two determinant equations and the $(0,0)$ entries of $AB=A$ and
$BA=B$ give, respectively,
\[
A_{01}A_{10}=B_{01}B_{10}=A_{01}B_{10}=B_{01}A_{10}=0.
\]
Hence $(A_{01}+B_{01})(A_{10}+B_{10})=0$, whereas both factors are
positive if $H_{01},H_{10}>0$. This excludes the singular-binary branch.

\subsection{The nonsingular classification and its resonance}
It remains to consider invertible $A,B,E,F,G$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:matrix-nonsingular-shape}
Let real invertible $2\times2$ matrices satisfy the exact swaps, and
suppose $\tr A,\tr B,\tr E>0$. If $A=B$, then $E=F=G$ and $AE=EA$.
If $A\ne B$, there are $\mu,t>0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nonsingular-digit-shape}
\begin{gathered}
\tr A=\tr B=3\mu,\qquad
\det A=\det B=2\mu^2,\\
A(B-A)=\mu(B-A),\qquad (B-A)A=2\mu(B-A),\\
E=t(2A-\mu I),\qquad
F=t(A+B-\mu I),\qquad G=t(2B-\mu I).
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
When $A=B$, cancellation of the invertible $A$ in $AE=EA=AF$ and
$AG=FA$ gives $E=F=G$.

Suppose $A\ne B$. Determinants give $\det A=\det B$.
The exact swaps also give
\[
(AB)E=EB^2,\qquad (BA)G=GA^2.
\]
Taking traces after cancellation of the invertible intertwiners, and
using $\tr(X^2)=(\tr X)^2-2\det X$, gives
\[
\tr A=\tr B,\qquad \tr(AB)=\tr(A^2).
\]
Thus $N=B-A\ne0$ again satisfies
$\tr N=\det N=\tr(AN)=0$, so $N^2=0$ and
\[
AN=aN,\qquad NA=\kappa N,\qquad a+\kappa=\tr A.
\]
Invertibility of $A$ makes $a,\kappa$ nonzero.
Moreover $A$ cannot be scalar: if it were, the exact swaps and
invertibility would successively give $G=F$, $E=F$, and $A=B$.
Its centralizer therefore gives $E=xI+yA$.

Put $b=x+ya$, $e=b/a$, and $g=e\kappa/a$.
The identities $AF=EB$ and $AG=FA$ give
\[
F=E+eN,\qquad G=E+gN.
\]
Substitute these formulas into $BE=FB$ and $BG=GB$ and use $N^2=0$.
Comparison of the coefficients of the nonzero matrix $N$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-resonance-equations}
y(\kappa-a)=e\kappa,\qquad
(g-y)(a-\kappa)=0,\qquad ga=e\kappa.
\end{equation}
If $a=\kappa$, the first equation makes $e=0$, hence $EN=0$,
contradicting invertibility of $E$. Therefore $g=y$.
Also $y\ne0$, since otherwise the same equations again give $EN=0$.
Equations~\eqref{eq:matrix-resonance-equations} now imply
\[
\kappa=2a,\qquad e=y/2,\qquad x=-ya/2.
\]
This is the $2:1$ resonance responsible for the nonscalar family.
Set $\mu=a$ and $t=y/2$. The positive trace of $A$ gives $\mu>0$;
$\tr E=4t\mu>0$ gives $t>0$. Applying Cayley--Hamilton for $A$ to
$N$ gives $\det A=2\mu^2$. Substitution yields all of
\eqref{eq:nonsingular-digit-shape}.
\end{proof}

First exclude the repeated case $A=B$, $E=F=G=T$.
Let $c$ be the first row of $C$, $d$ the first column of $D$, and
$z=Ad-d\ge0$. The boundary comparisons imply
\[
cA^2d\le cTd\le cAd,\qquad\text{so}\qquad cAz\le0.
\]
Nonnegativity, $c_0>0$, and $A_{00}\ge1$ give
$z_0=0$ and $A_{01}z_1=0$. Write
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\r&s\end{pmatrix},\qquad
T=\begin{pmatrix}e&f\\g&h\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Since $d_0>0$, $z_0=(a-1)d_0+bd_1=0$ gives $a=1$ and $bd_1=0$.
Then $bz_1=0$ gives $br=0$.
The first coordinate of $Td\le Ad$, with $e\ge1$, gives $fd_1=0$.
If $H_{01}=2b+3f>0$, we obtain $d_1=0$.
The second coordinate gives $g\le r$, and $H_{10}=2r+3g>0$ makes
$r>0$. Hence $b=0$ and $f>0$.
But the $(0,0)$ entry of $AT=TA$ says $bg=fr$, a contradiction.

For the unequal case, put
\[
U=A-\mu I,\qquad V=B-\mu I,\qquad S=U+V.
\]
These matrices are nonnegative. Indeed, if the diagonal entries of $A$
are $p,q$, then $p+q=3\mu$ and
\[
(p-\mu)(q-\mu)=A_{01}A_{10}\ge0.
\]
Their sum is $\mu>0$, so both $p-\mu,q-\mu$ are nonnegative.
The same argument applies to $B$. The classification gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-shifted-products}
\tr U=\tr V=\mu,\qquad
U^2=UV=\mu U,\qquad VU=V^2=\mu V.
\end{equation}
In particular $SU=SV=\mu S$ and $S^2=2\mu S$.
The off-diagonal entries of $H$ are $(1+3t)$ times those of $S$.
Thus $S_{01},S_{10}>0$, and the $(0,0)$ entry of $S^2=2\mu S$
also gives $S_{00}>0$.

Multiply $BD\ge GD$ on the left by $S$. Since
$SB=2\mu S$, $SG=3t\mu S$, and $(SD)_{00}>0$, we obtain
$3t\le2$. Now put
\[
u=(CU)_{00},\qquad v=(CV)_{00}.
\]
They are nonnegative and $u+v=(CS)_{00}>0$.
Multiplying $CE\ge CB$ on the right by $U$, and $CG\ge CAB$ on the
right by $V$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-boundary-resonance}
u+v\le3tu,\qquad 2\mu(u+v)\le3tv.
\end{equation}
For example, the products used here are
$BU=\mu S$, $EU=3t\mu U$, $ABV=2\mu^2S$, and $GV=3t\mu V$;
these follow directly from \eqref{eq:matrix-shifted-products}.
Together with $3t\le2$, \eqref{eq:matrix-boundary-resonance} gives
$v\le u$ and $\mu(u+v)\le v$, hence $\mu\le1/2$.
On the other hand, $A_{00}=U_{00}+\mu\ge1$ and
$U_{00}+U_{11}=\mu$ give $2\mu\ge1$.
Therefore $\mu=1/2$ and $U_{11}=V_{11}=0$.
The $(1,1)$ entry of $S^2=2\mu S$ now reads
$S_{10}S_{01}=0$, again a contradiction.

\subsection{The first diagonals in the triangular cases}
We have excluded $H_{01},H_{10}>0$ in every determinant case.
Nonnegativity therefore makes one off-diagonal entry zero in every
digit matrix. This proves the common triangular orientation.
The diagonal equalities obtained from the trace argument reduce, in
either triangular orientation, to
\[
A_{00}G_{00}=F_{00}A_{00},\qquad
B_{00}E_{00}=F_{00}B_{00},\qquad
A_{00}F_{00}=E_{00}B_{00}.
\]
The positive first-diagonal bounds allow cancellation, so write
\[
A_{00}=B_{00}=t\ge1,\qquad
E_{00}=F_{00}=G_{00}=s\ge1.
\]
For completeness, the following entry arguments show why the boundary
comparisons force $t=s=1$, even when secondary entries vanish.

For upper triangular digits, write
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}t&\alpha\\0&u\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{pmatrix}t&\beta\\0&v\end{pmatrix},\quad
G=\begin{pmatrix}s&\gamma\\0&y\end{pmatrix},
\]
and let $(c_0,c_1)$ be the first row of $C$, $(d_0,d_1)^\mathsf T$
the first column of $D$. Thus $c_0,d_0\ge1$ and all other displayed
entries are nonnegative. The $(0,0)$ entry of $CG\ge CAB$ gives
$s\ge t^2$.
If $s>t$, the first coordinate of $BD\ge GD$ gives
$d_1>0$ and $\beta>\gamma$. The second coordinates of
$AD\ge D$ and $BD\ge GD$ give $u\ge1$ and $v\ge y$.
The $(0,1)$ entry of $CG\ge CAB$ is
\[
c_0(t\beta+\alpha v)+c_1uv\le c_0\gamma+c_1y.
\]
Since $uv\ge y$, it implies $\beta\le t\beta+\alpha v\le\gamma$,
a contradiction. Thus $t^2\le s\le t$, proving $t=s=1$.

For lower triangular digits, write
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
A=\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\\alpha&u\end{pmatrix},&
B=\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\\beta&v\end{pmatrix},&
E=\begin{pmatrix}s&0\\\varepsilon&w\end{pmatrix},\\[4pt]
F=\begin{pmatrix}s&0\\\phi&x\end{pmatrix},&
G=\begin{pmatrix}s&0\\\gamma&y\end{pmatrix}.&
\end{array}
\]
Now $BD\ge GD$ gives $s\le t$. Suppose $t>1$, so $t^2>s$.
The $(0,0)$ entries of $CF\ge CA^2$, $CG\ge CAB$, and $CE\ge CB$
give
\[
c_1>0,\qquad \phi>(t+u)\alpha\ge\alpha,\qquad
\gamma>t\alpha+u\beta,\qquad \beta\le\varepsilon.
\]
Their $(0,1)$ entries give $v\le w$, $u^2\le x$, and $uv\le y$.
If $u=0$, the $(1,0)$ swap comparison $AG\ge FA$ gives
$t\phi+x\alpha\le s\alpha$, contradicting $\phi>\alpha$ and
$t\ge s$.

If $u>0$, then $x>0$. The diagonal swap equalities
$uy=xu$, $vx=yu$, and $ux=wv$ give
$y=w=x$ and $v=u$; hence $x\ge u$.
The $(1,0)$ entry of $BD\ge GD$ gives $\gamma\le\beta$.
Together with $\gamma>t\alpha+u\beta$, this forces $u<1$.
The $(1,0)$ entry of $AE\ge EA$ now yields
\[
(t-u)\varepsilon\le(s-x)\alpha
 \le(t-u)\alpha,
\]
so $\varepsilon\le\alpha$ and therefore
$\gamma\le\beta\le\varepsilon\le\alpha$.
Finally $AG\ge FA$ gives
\[
t\phi+x\alpha\le s\alpha+u\gamma
 \le(s+u)\alpha,
\]
and $x\ge u$, $s\le t$ imply $\phi\le\alpha$, a contradiction.
Thus $t\le1$, completing the lower triangular case.

\begin{proof}[Completion of the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:matrix}]
If both off-diagonals of $H$ were positive, the trace argument would
give the exact swaps. The singular-middle, singular-binary, repeated,
and unequal nonsingular arguments above exhaust the determinant and
equality cases and exclude that possibility. The final subsection then
gives the common triangular orientation and the five unit first
diagonals.

For reversed rules, transpose every symbol matrix. Since
$(XY)^\mathsf T=Y^\mathsf TX^\mathsf T$, the reversed matrix comparisons
become exactly \eqref{eq:swaps} and \eqref{eq:matrix-boundaries}, with
the same symbol names. Transposition preserves nonnegativity and each
$(0,0)$ entry, and exchanges upper and lower triangularity.
The forward conclusion therefore proves the reversed conclusion as
well.
\end{proof}

\section{The lower triangular affine case}
After Lemma~\ref{lem:matrix}, lower triangular digit maps have the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower}
F_s(x,y)=(x+h_s,\ q_sx+z_sy+k_s),\qquad h_s,q_s,z_s,k_s\ge0.
\end{equation}
Their first offsets add under composition. The six weak swap gaps sum
to zero, so all six vanish and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twooffsets}
h_a=h_b=r,\qquad h_e=h_f=h_g=u,\qquad r,u\ge0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Scalar classifications used in the lower case}
The scalar statements below also handle zero slopes. They are stated
with the inequalities actually used, so their application does not
require a scalar version of the diagonal lower bound.

\begin{lemma}[Forward scalar classification]\label{lem:scalar-forward}
Let \(a,b,e,f,g,\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon,\varphi,\gamma,\delta\)
be nonnegative real numbers. Suppose
\begin{gather}
b\le e,\qquad a^2\le f,\qquad
af\ge eb,\quad ag\ge fa,\quad be\ge fb,\quad bf\ge ga,
\label{eq:scalar-matrix}\\
\begin{aligned}
b\delta+\beta&\ge g\delta+\gamma,&
a\varepsilon+\alpha&\ge e\alpha+\varepsilon,\\
a\varphi+\alpha&\ge e\beta+\varepsilon,&
a\gamma+\alpha&\ge f\alpha+\varphi,\\
b\varepsilon+\beta&\ge f\beta+\varphi,&
b\varphi+\beta&\ge g\alpha+\gamma,
\end{aligned}\label{eq:scalar-offset}\\
\varepsilon\ge\beta,\qquad
\varphi\ge(a+1)\alpha,\qquad
\gamma\ge a\beta+\alpha.\label{eq:scalar-boundary}
\end{gather}
Then either all five offsets
\(\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon,\varphi,\gamma\) are zero, or all five slopes
\(a,b,e,f,g\) are zero and all five offsets are equal and positive.
In particular,
\[
\varepsilon=\beta,\qquad
\varphi=(a+1)\alpha,\qquad
\gamma=a\beta+\alpha.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The four nonnegative swap differences in
\eqref{eq:scalar-matrix} sum to zero. Hence
\(af=eb\), \(ag=fa\), \(be=fb\), and \(bf=ga\).

If \(a=0\), then \(eb=0\) and \(e\ge b\) give \(b=0\).
The \(ag\) offset comparison and
\(\varphi\ge\alpha\) give \(\varphi=\alpha\).
The \(be\) comparison then gives \(\beta\ge\alpha\).
On the other hand the \(ae\) comparison gives
\(\alpha\ge e\alpha+\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon\ge\beta\).
Thus \(\alpha=\beta=\varepsilon=\varphi\).
The \(bf\) comparison and \(\gamma\ge\alpha\) also give
\(\gamma=\alpha\). If this common value is positive, the \(ae,ag,bf\)
comparisons force \(e=f=g=0\). This proves the assertion in this branch.

If \(a>0\), the inequality \(f\ge a^2\) gives \(f>0\);
\(af=eb\) then gives \(b>0\). The swap equalities force
\[
a=b=:t>0,\qquad e=f=g=:s,\qquad s\ge t,\quad s\ge t^2.
\]
The first inequality in \eqref{eq:scalar-offset} implies
\(\gamma\le\beta\), since \((s-t)\delta\ge0\).
If \(t\ge1\), the chain
\[
\beta\ge\gamma\ge t\beta+\alpha\ge\beta+\alpha
\]
gives \(\alpha=0\), \(\gamma=\beta=t\beta\).
The \(ag\) comparison gives \(\varphi\le\beta\), while \(af\) gives
\(s\beta+\varepsilon\le t\varphi\le t\beta=\beta\).
Because \(s\ge t\ge1\), this forces
\(\varepsilon=0\), hence \(\beta=\gamma=\varphi=0\).

If \(t<1\), the \(ae\) comparison gives
\((1-t)\varepsilon\le(1-s)\alpha\le(1-t)\alpha\).
Consequently
\(\beta\le\varepsilon\le\alpha\le\gamma\le\beta\).
All these quantities are equal, and
\(\gamma\ge t\beta+\alpha\) with \(t>0\) forces their common value
to be zero. The \(ag\) comparison finally gives \(\varphi=0\).
\end{proof}

For application to maps \(y\mapsto z_sy+q_s\), the five slopes above are
\(z_a,z_b,z_e,z_f,z_g\) and the offsets are
\(q_a,q_b,q_e,q_f,q_g\). The dynamic comparison is evaluated at the
nonnegative inner-boundary value \(\delta\). The same statement applies
with \(k_s\) in place of \(q_s\).

\begin{lemma}[Reversed scalar cancellation]\label{lem:scalar-reversed}
Let \(a\ge1\) and let \(b,f,g,\alpha,\beta,\varphi,\gamma,\xi\)
be nonnegative. Suppose
\begin{gather*}
g\le b,\qquad \gamma\le\beta,\qquad af\le ag,\\
b(a\xi+\alpha)+\beta\le g\xi+\gamma,\\
a\varphi+\alpha\le g\alpha+\gamma,\qquad
a\gamma+\alpha\le f\beta+\varphi.
\end{gather*}
Then \(\alpha=0\) and \(\beta=\gamma\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first offset inequality says
\[
(ba-g)\xi+b\alpha+(\beta-\gamma)\le0.
\]
All three terms are nonnegative, so \(b\alpha=0\) and
\(\beta=\gamma\). If \(\alpha>0\), then \(b=0\);
\(g\le b\) and \(af\le ag\) give \(g=f=0\).
The remaining inequalities become
\(a\varphi+\alpha\le\gamma\) and
\(a\gamma+\alpha\le\varphi\). Their sum contradicts \(a\ge1\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Exceptional reversed scalar shape]\label{lem:scalar-reversed-shape}
Let \(a\ge1\), and let the other variables below be nonnegative.
Assume
\[
g\le b,\qquad af=eb,\qquad ag=fa,\qquad be=fb,
\]
and
\[
b\varepsilon+\beta\le\varphi,\qquad
a\varphi\le\beta,\qquad
a\varepsilon\le\varepsilon,\qquad
b\varphi+\beta\le e\beta+\varepsilon.
\]
Then either \(\beta=\varepsilon=\varphi=0\), or
\[
a=1,\qquad b=f=g=0,\qquad\varphi=\beta.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first two offset inequalities give
\(\varphi=\beta\) and \(b\varepsilon=0\).
If \(a>1\), \(a\varphi\le\beta=\varphi\) and
\(a\varepsilon\le\varepsilon\) give the zero case.
If \(a=1\) and \(b>0\), then \(\varepsilon=0\) and
\(be=fb\) gives \(e=f=g\le b\). The last inequality then yields
\(b\beta+\beta\le e\beta\le b\beta\), so \(\beta=\varphi=0\).
If \(a=1,b=0\), the equalities \(af=eb\) and \(ag=fa\)
give \(f=g=0\), which is the exceptional case.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Application to the two-coordinate maps}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:lower}
For lower digit maps \eqref{eq:lower}, every first-offset gap vanishes in
either orientation, with arbitrary admissible boundary maps.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Forward orientation]
The dynamic rule $bd\to gd$ gives $r\ge u$. Write the first row of $C$
as $(c_0,c_1)$, with $c_0\ge1$. If $c_1=0$, the $cf$ boundary gives
$u\ge2r$, hence $r=u=0$, and every boundary gap is zero.

Suppose $c_1>0$. The lower rows of the digit matrix products define
scalar affine maps $y\mapsto z_sy+q_s$. The matrix inequalities induce
all the forward scalar constraints, with outer boundary the identity and
inner boundary the constant $D_{10}/D_{00}$. Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar-forward} gives precisely two possibilities: every $q_s=0$, or
every $q_s=v>0$ and every $z_s=0$.

If every $q_s=0$, apply Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar-forward} to
$y\mapsto z_sy+k_s$. Put
\[
\Delta_e=k_e-k_b,\quad
\Delta_f=k_f-(1+z_a)k_a,\quad
\Delta_g=k_g-k_a-z_ak_b.
\]
The original boundary offset inequalities give
$c_1\Delta_e\ge c_0(r-u)$ and
$c_1\Delta_f,c_1\Delta_g\ge c_0(2r-u)$.
Since $r\ge u$, these supply the remaining scalar weak constraints.
Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar-forward} makes all three $\Delta$'s zero. Hence $u\ge2r$,
so $r=u=0$ and all eleven first-offset gaps vanish.

In the other case, $q_s=v>0$ and $z_s=0$. The second-offset $ag$
inequality and first-offset $cf$ inequality yield
\[
k_a-k_f\ge v(r-u),\qquad
k_f-k_a\ge vr+(c_0/c_1)(2r-u).
\]
Their sum forces $r=u=0$. The remaining weak inequalities make all five
$k_s$ equal, so the three boundary gaps vanish as well.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Reversed orientation]
The first-offset swap equalities still give \eqref{eq:twooffsets}; now
the inner boundary $fc\to aac$ gives $u\ge2r$. If $D_{01}=0$, the
outer dynamic comparison gives $r\ge u$, and the result follows.
For $D_{01}>0$, the two dynamic matrix comparisons give
\(z_a\ge1\), \(z_g\le z_b\), and \(q_g\le q_b\).
Write \(c_0=C_{00}>0\), \(c_1=C_{10}\ge0\).
The \(gc\) matrix comparison reads
\[
z_b(z_ac_1+q_ac_0)+q_bc_0\le z_gc_1+q_gc_0.
\]
Together with the \(ga,fb\) matrix comparisons, this is
Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar-reversed} with
\[
(\alpha,\beta,\varphi,\gamma,\xi)
=(q_ac_0,q_bc_0,q_fc_0,q_gc_0,c_1).
\]
It gives \(q_a=0\), \(q_b=q_g\).
The remaining \(ea,fa,ga,eb\) comparisons now supply
Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar-reversed-shape}. Thus either all \(q_s=0\), or
\[
z_a=1,\quad z_b=z_f=z_g=0,\quad
q_a=0,\quad q_b=q_f=q_g=\beta\ge0.
\]
No claim about \(z_e\) or \(q_e\) is needed in the exceptional case.

In the zero case, the \(db\) offset comparison and \(u\ge2r\) give
\(k_g\le k_b\). Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar-reversed} again,
now to \((\alpha,\beta,\varphi,\gamma,\xi)
=(k_a,k_b,k_f,k_g,(v_c)_1)\).
It gives \(k_a=0\) and \(k_b=k_g\), so \(db\) forces \(r\ge u\).
Hence \(r=u=0\).

In the exceptional case, the \(gb\) comparison gives
\[
k_g-k_b\ge q_b(u-r)\ge0.
\]
Together with
\(D_{00}u+D_{01}k_g\le D_{00}r+D_{01}k_b\),
this forces \(r=u=0\) and \(k_b=k_g\).
The \(ga,fb\) offset comparisons reduce to
\(k_f+k_a\le k_g\) and \(k_g+k_a\le k_f\);
therefore \(k_a=0\).
The dynamic gaps are now zero. The six swap and three inner-boundary
first-offset gaps depend only on \(r,u\), so they vanish as well.
\end{proof}


\section{Upper normalization and aggregate cancellation}
\label{sec:upper-normalization}
Upper digit maps with unit first diagonals have the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper}
F_s(x,y)=(x+q_sy+h_s,\ z_sy+k_s),\qquad q_s,z_s,h_s,k_s\ge0.
\end{equation}
In the forward orientation, replace the boundary maps by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal}
\widehat C(x,y)=(x+\kappa y,0),\quad
\widehat D(x,y)=(x,\tau),\qquad
\kappa=C_{01}/C_{00},\quad \tau=(v_d)_1.
\end{equation}
The outer boundary matrix and offset comparisons in coordinate zero
are divided by $C_{00}>0$. The dynamic offset differences are unchanged,
and their normalized matrix differences are zero. Thus all weak rules
are preserved, and zero first-offset gaps in the normalized model imply
zero gaps in the original model. This leaves 22 nonnegative parameters.

For a digit word, composition in \eqref{eq:upper} is described exactly by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:composition}
\begin{aligned}
q_{uv}&=q_v+q_uz_v,& z_{uv}&=z_uz_v,\\
h_{uv}&=h_u+h_v+q_uk_v,& k_{uv}&=k_u+z_uk_v.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In particular, the products $q_uk_v$ remain in every offset comparison.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:aggregate}
Put
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
p=z_a+z_b-2,& Q=q_a+q_b,& R=k_a+k_b,\\
r=z_e+z_f+z_g-3,& P=q_e+q_f+q_g,& K=k_e+k_f+k_g.
\end{array}
\]
The sums of the six swap gaps in the $q,k,h$ coordinates are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aggregate}
G_q=rQ-pP\ge0,\quad G_k=pK-rR\ge0,\quad G_h=QK-PR\ge0.
\end{equation}
If $p<0$ or $r<0$, and $Q+P,R+K>0$, all six affine swaps are exact.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Expanding \eqref{eq:composition} gives \eqref{eq:aggregate} and
\[
RG_q+QG_k=pG_h,\qquad KG_q+PG_k=rG_h.
\]
A negative coefficient on the right forces $G_h=0$. Adding the resulting
equalities gives $(R+K)G_q+(Q+P)G_k=0$, hence $G_q=G_k=0$.
Every individual gap in these sums is nonnegative. The secondary-slope
swap gaps also sum to zero, completing the assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Degenerate off-diagonal or offset data]\label{lem:upper-degenerate}
For a normalized upper model satisfying all eleven weak rules,
either of the conditions
\[
q_a=q_b=q_e=q_f=q_g=0
\quad\text{or}\quad
k_a=k_b=k_e=k_f=k_g=0
\]
implies that all eleven first-offset gaps vanish.
There is no additional condition on the secondary slopes \(z_s\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If all \(q_s=0\), the first offsets add, so the four nontrivial swap
inequalities give \(h_a=h_b=r\) and \(h_e=h_f=h_g=u\).
The dynamic comparison gives \(r\ge u\).
For \(\kappa=0\), the \(cf\) comparison gives \(u\ge2r\),
hence \(r=u=0\). For \(\kappa>0\), the \(ce,cf\) matrix
comparisons give \(z_e\ge z_b\), \(z_f\ge z_a^2\).
The boundary offset comparisons give
\[
\kappa(k_e-k_b)\ge r-u,\qquad
\kappa\bigl(k_f-(1+z_a)k_a\bigr)\ge2r-u,
\]
and the analogous inequality for \(k_g-k_a-z_ak_b\).
These supply the boundary hypotheses of
Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar-forward} for the five maps
\(y\mapsto z_sy+k_s\), with inner value \(\delta=\tau\).
Its three zero-gap conclusions again give \(u\ge2r\).
Thus \(r=u=0\), and the scalar equalities make the three outer gaps zero.

If all \(k_s=0\), the same swap argument gives the two values \(r,u\).
Now \(cf\) gives \(u\ge2r\).
When \(\tau=0\), the dynamic comparison gives \(r\ge u\).
When \(\tau>0\), its matrix and offset components imply
\[
z_a\ge1,\qquad z_g\le z_b,\qquad q_g\le q_b.
\]
The \(q\)-composition rule in \eqref{eq:composition} reverses the order
of scalar affine composition. Thus the relevant \(q\)-inequalities are
exactly those of Lemma~\ref{lem:scalar-reversed}, with
\[
(a,b,f,g,\alpha,\beta,\varphi,\gamma,\xi)
=(z_a,z_b,z_f,z_g,q_a,q_b,q_f,q_g,\kappa).
\]
For example the \(cg\) matrix comparison is
\[
z_b(z_a\kappa+q_a)+q_b\le z_g\kappa+q_g,
\]
and the \(ag,bf\) comparisons give its two remaining offset inequalities.
The lemma gives \(q_a=0\) and \(q_b=q_g\).
Consequently \(bd\) gives \(r\ge u\), and again \(r=u=0\).
Substitution in \eqref{eq:composition} now makes all eleven gaps zero.
\end{proof}

The remaining case has positive total \(q\) and positive total \(k\).
The next lemmas identify what makes the common-form branches impossible.

\begin{lemma}[Projection of exact swaps]\label{lem:projected-offsets}
Suppose a normalized upper model satisfies all weak rules and its six
affine swaps are exact. If \(\lambda\ge0\) and
\[
q_s=\lambda(1-z_s)\qquad(s=a,b,e,f,g),
\]
put \(H_s=h_s+\lambda k_s\). Then
\[
H_a=H_b=:L,\qquad H_e=H_f=H_g=:N,\qquad 0\le N\le L.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The nonnegative row \(\ell(x,y)=x+\lambda y\) satisfies
\[
\ell(F_s(x,y))=\ell(x,y)+H_s.
\]
Apply \(\ell\) to the exact \(ag,be,af\) swaps, in that order.
Their constant terms give \(H_g=H_f\), \(H_e=H_f\), and \(H_a=H_b\).
Applying \(\ell\) to the weak dynamic comparison
\(F_b\widehat D\ge F_g\widehat D\) gives \(L\ge N\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Common fixed height]\label{lem:common-form-zero}
Under the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:projected-offsets},
assume \(\lambda>0\) and
\(k_s=\eta(1-z_s)\) for every digit, where \(\eta\ge0\).
Then all five \(k_s\) are zero.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every digit fixes the height \(y=\eta\). At this height,
\[
F_s(x,\eta)=(x+H_s,\eta),
\]
because \(q_s\eta=\lambda k_s\).
Evaluate the weak \(cf\) boundary comparison at \((0,\eta)\).
The common term \(\kappa\eta\) cancels, leaving \(N\ge2L\).
Together with \(0\le N\le L\), this gives \(L=N=0\).
Each \(H_s=h_s+\lambda k_s\) is a sum of nonnegative terms and
\(\lambda>0\), so every \(k_s=0\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Matching boundary projection]\label{lem:matching-boundary-zero}
Under the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:projected-offsets},
if \(\lambda>0\) and \(\kappa=\lambda\), then all five \(k_s\) are zero.
No common-form hypothesis on the \(k_s\) is needed.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first component of \(\widehat C\) is now \(\ell\).
The \(cf\) offset comparison therefore gives \(N\ge2L\) directly.
The last part of the preceding proof applies.
\end{proof}


\section{Completion of the upper case and word reversal}
\label{sec:upper-completion}
We continue with the normalized maps \eqref{eq:upper}--\eqref{eq:normal}.
All their parameters are nonnegative. The first diagonal of every digit
matrix is one; no positivity is assumed for its secondary slope $z_s$.
The next lemma explains the two exceptional slope pairs that occur in
the proof.

\begin{lemma}[Triangular resonance]\label{lem:triangular-resonance}
Let $\alpha,\beta,\rho>0$, and let $\sigma,a,b,e,f,g$ be real numbers.
Consider two upper triangular matrices with common diagonal
$(\alpha,\rho)$ and upper entries $a,b$, and three with common diagonal
$(\beta,\sigma)$ and upper entries $e,f,g$. Their six forward swap
equalities on the upper entries are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:resonance-six}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha e+a\sigma&=\beta a+e\rho,&
\alpha f+a\sigma&=\beta b+e\rho,\\
\alpha g+a\sigma&=\beta a+f\rho,&
\alpha e+b\sigma&=\beta b+f\rho,\\
\alpha f+b\sigma&=\beta a+g\rho,&
\alpha g+b\sigma&=\beta b+g\rho.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These equations hold if and only if at least one of the following
descriptions holds:
\begin{align}
&a=b,\qquad e=f=g,\qquad
  (\alpha-\rho)e=(\beta-\sigma)a;
  \label{eq:resonance-common}\\
&\rho=2\alpha,\qquad \sigma=3\beta,\qquad
  \alpha e=2\beta a,\quad
  \alpha f=\beta(a+b),\quad
  \alpha g=2\beta b.
  \label{eq:resonance-exception}
\end{align}
The two descriptions may overlap. In particular, if $a\ne b$, the
exceptional diagonal ratios in \eqref{eq:resonance-exception} are
necessary. No sign assumption on $\sigma$ or on the five upper entries
is needed for this algebraic statement.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $d=b-a$, $u=f-e$, and $v=g-e$. Subtracting the first equation in
\eqref{eq:resonance-six} from the second, fourth, third, and sixth,
respectively, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:resonance-differences}
\alpha u=\beta d,\qquad
(\sigma-\beta)d=\rho u,\qquad
\alpha v=\rho u,\qquad
(\alpha-\rho)v=(\beta-\sigma)d.
\end{equation}
If $d=0$, the first and third identities, together with
$\alpha>0$, give $u=v=0$. The first equation of
\eqref{eq:resonance-six} is then the relation in
\eqref{eq:resonance-common}.

If $d\ne0$, positivity of $\alpha,\beta,\rho$ makes both $u$ and $v$
nonzero. The second and fourth identities give
$(\alpha-\rho)v=-\rho u$; using the third yields
$(2\alpha-\rho)v=0$. Thus $\rho=2\alpha$. The first two identities
now give $(\sigma-3\beta)d=0$, so $\sigma=3\beta$.
Substitution into the first three original equations gives
$\alpha e=2\beta a$, $\alpha f=\beta(a+b)$, and
$\alpha g=2\beta b$. Finally, substitution of either
\eqref{eq:resonance-common} or \eqref{eq:resonance-exception} verifies
all six equations, including the fifth equation, which was not needed
to derive the alternatives.
\end{proof}

For the $q$ entries in \eqref{eq:upper}, equal binary secondary slopes
$t>0$ and equal ternary secondary slopes $s\ge0$ give
$(\alpha,\beta,\rho,\sigma)=(1,1,t,s)$ in the lemma. Their exceptional
pair is therefore $(t,s)=(2,3)$. For the $k$ entries, use the homogeneous
matrices of the scalar maps $y\mapsto z_sy+k_s$. Here the four diagonal
parameters are $(t,s,1,1)$, so the exceptional pair is
$(t,s)=(1/2,1/3)$. The latter use requires $t,s>0$; zero slopes will be
handled separately rather than divided out.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:upper}
Every normalized upper model satisfying all eleven weak forward rules
has zero first-offset gap in every rule.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If all $q_s$ or all $k_s$ vanish, Lemma~\ref{lem:upper-degenerate}
applies. Hence it suffices to rule out a model with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-positive-totals}
Q+P>0,\qquad R+K>0,
\end{equation}
using the notation of Lemma~\ref{lem:aggregate}. We divide the proof
according to the secondary slopes.

\smallskip\noindent
\emph{1. Both binary slopes are positive.}
The secondary-slope swap equalities are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-slope-equalities}
z_a z_f=z_e z_b,\qquad z_a z_g=z_f z_a,\qquad
z_b z_e=z_f z_b,\qquad z_b z_f=z_g z_a.
\end{equation}
Thus $z_e=z_f=z_g=s\ge0$. If $s>0$, the first equality also gives
$z_a=z_b=t>0$.

First suppose $s>0$ and $t<1$ or $s<1$. Then
$p=2(t-1)<0$ or $r=3(s-1)<0$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:aggregate} makes
all six affine swaps exact. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:triangular-resonance}
to their $q$ entries. The exceptional pair $(2,3)$ is incompatible with
the present case. Consequently
\[
q_a=q_b=x,\qquad q_e=q_f=q_g=y,\qquad
(1-t)y=(1-s)x.
\]
Here $x,y\ge0$ and $x+y>0$. These conditions give a parameter
$\lambda>0$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-common-q}
q_s=\lambda(1-z_s)\qquad(s\in\{a,b,e,f,g\}).
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $t<1$, then $x>0$ and one can take
$\lambda=x/(1-t)$; otherwise $s<1$, $y>0$, and one takes
$\lambda=y/(1-s)$.

The resonance lemma applied to the $k$ entries now gives either the
same common form $k_s=\eta(1-z_s)$, with $\eta>0$ by
\eqref{eq:upper-positive-totals}, or $(t,s)=(1/2,1/3)$.
The common-form branch is excluded by Lemma~\ref{lem:common-form-zero}.
In the exceptional branch, the matrix gaps of $ce\to cb$ and
$cf\to caa$, after substituting \eqref{eq:upper-common-q}, are
\[
\frac{\lambda-\kappa}{6}\ge0,
\qquad
\frac{\kappa-\lambda}{12}\ge0.
\]
Thus $\kappa=\lambda$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:matching-boundary-zero}
forces every $k_s$ to vanish. This also contradicts
\eqref{eq:upper-positive-totals}. The exceptional resonance therefore
does not leave an untreated family of offsets.

It remains, for $s>0$, to consider $s,t\ge1$. If $s>t$, the
secondary-offset comparisons for $bd\to gd$ and $bg\to gb$ give
\[
k_b\ge k_g+(s-t)\tau\ge k_g,
\qquad (t-1)k_g\ge(s-1)k_b.
\]
Since $s-1>t-1\ge0$, these force $k_b=k_g=\tau=0$.
The $ag\to fa$ comparison becomes
$(1-s)k_a\ge k_f$, so $k_a=k_f=0$; $af\to eb$ then gives $k_e=0$.

If $1\le s\le t$ and $t>1$, the three boundary matrix comparisons give
\[
q_e\ge q_b,\qquad q_f\ge(1+t)q_a,\qquad
q_g\ge q_b+tq_a.
\]
The discarded terms are nonnegative multiples of $t-s$ or $t^2-s$.
In particular $P\ge2Q$. On the other hand, the aggregate $q$ inequality
is
\[
2(t-1)P\le3(s-1)Q\le3(t-1)Q.
\]
Dividing by $t-1>0$ gives $P\le3Q/2$. Thus $Q=P=0$, contrary to
\eqref{eq:upper-positive-totals}.

For the remaining unit case $s=t=1$, the linear swap inequalities in
the $q$ and $k$ coordinates force
\[
q_a=q_b=q,\quad q_e=q_f=q_g=p_0,\qquad
k_a=k_b=k,\quad k_e=k_f=k_g=\ell.
\]
The boundary matrix comparison $cf\to caa$, the secondary-offset
comparison $bd\to gd$, and the first-offset comparison $ae\to ea$
then yield, respectively,
\[
p_0\ge2q,\qquad k\ge\ell,\qquad p_0k\le q\ell.
\]
Hence $2qk\le p_0k\le q\ell\le qk$, and therefore $qk=p_0k=0$.
Positive total $q$ implies $p_0>0$, whereas positive total $k$ implies
$k>0$. This contradiction completes the unit case. Without the
positive-total assumption, these same inequalities place the model
in one of the two degenerate cases of
Lemma~\ref{lem:upper-degenerate}.

Finally, suppose $s=0$. The binary slopes $z_a,z_b$ may now differ.
Since $r=-3<0$, all swaps are exact. The $af,be$ and $ag,bf$ pairs
give
\[
(1+z_b)(q_f-q_e)=0,\qquad
(1+z_a)(q_g-q_f)=0.
\]
Thus $q_e=q_f=q_g=\lambda$, and the $ae,bg$ equalities give
$q_a=\lambda(1-z_a)$ and $q_b=\lambda(1-z_b)$.
Positive total $q$ makes $\lambda>0$.
The corresponding $k$ equalities give
\[
k_e=k_f=k_g=\eta,\qquad
k_a=\eta(1-z_a),\qquad k_b=\eta(1-z_b).
\]
For example, subtracting the $ae$ and $af$ equalities gives
$z_a(k_f-k_e)=0$, and then $ag$ gives $k_g=k_e$.
Here $z_a>0$ is available. Positive total $k$ makes $\eta>0$,
so Lemma~\ref{lem:common-form-zero} again applies.

\smallskip\noindent
\emph{2. At least one binary slope is zero.}
Consider first $z_a=0$ and $z_b>1$. Equations
\eqref{eq:upper-slope-equalities} give $z_e=z_f=0$.
The $af$ and $be$ matrix comparisons become
\[
q_f\ge q_b+z_bq_e,
\qquad q_e\ge q_b+z_bq_f.
\]
Their sum gives $(1-z_b)(q_e+q_f)\ge2q_b$; nonnegativity and
$z_b>1$ force $q_b=q_e=q_f=0$. The $ae$ and $bg$ comparisons then
force $q_a=q_g=0$.
For $z_b=0$ and $z_a>1$, the slope equalities instead give
$z_f=z_g=0$. Now $ag$ and $bf$ yield
\[
q_g\ge q_a+z_aq_f,
\qquad q_f\ge q_a+z_aq_g,
\]
forcing $q_a=q_f=q_g=0$; $af$ and $ae$ then give $q_b=q_e=0$.
Both cases contradict positive total $q$.

In the remaining zero-slope cases $z_a+z_b\le1$, so
$p=z_a+z_b-2<0$ and the swaps are exact. If exactly one binary slope
is zero, their solutions have the common forms in the following table:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Binary slopes & Forced ternary zeros & Parameters in the common forms\\
\midrule
$z_a=0<z_b$ & $z_e=z_f=0$ & $\lambda=q_a$, $\eta=k_a$\\
$z_b=0<z_a$ & $z_f=z_g=0$ & $\lambda=q_b$, $\eta=k_b$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For clarity, the first row means
\[
q_e=q_f=q_a,\quad q_b=q_a(1-z_b),\quad q_g=q_a(1-z_g),
\]
and the same identities with $q$ replaced by $k$.
The equality $q_e=q_a$ follows from $ae$; the $af,be$ pair gives
$(1+z_b)(q_f-q_a)=0$, and $ag$ gives the formula for $q_g$.
The exact secondary-offset equations give the displayed $k$ identities.
For the second row one obtains
\[
q_f=q_g=q_b,\quad q_a=q_b(1-z_a),\quad q_e=q_b(1-z_e),
\]
again with the same $k$ identities. Here $af$ gives $q_f=q_b$, the
$ag,bf$ pair gives $(1+z_a)(q_g-q_b)=0$, and $be$ determines $q_e$.
Thus these conclusions use the actual forward equations in each case;
no exchange of symbol labels is needed. Both parameters are positive
under \eqref{eq:upper-positive-totals}, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:common-form-zero} excludes both rows of the table.

It remains to set $z_a=z_b=0$. The exact $af,bf$ matrix equations give
$(1+z_f)(q_a-q_b)=0$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-double-zero-q}
q_a=q_b=q>0,\qquad q_s=q(1-z_s)\quad(s=e,f,g).
\end{equation}
The six secondary-offset equations imply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-double-zero-k}
z_e(k_a-k_b)=0,\qquad
(1-z_f)(k_a-k_b)=0,\qquad
z_g(k_a-k_b)=0.
\end{equation}
If $k_a=k_b$, they give the common form with $\eta=k_a$, already
excluded. Otherwise \eqref{eq:upper-double-zero-k} forces exactly
\[
(z_a,z_b,z_e,z_f,z_g)=(0,0,0,1,0),\qquad
k_e=k_a,\quad k_f=0,\quad k_g=k_b.
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:upper-double-zero-q} gives
$q_a=q_b=q_e=q_g=q$ and $q_f=0$.
The matrix comparison $cf\to caa$ now says $\kappa\ge q$;
its offset comparison says
\[
h_f\ge2h_a+(q+\kappa)k_a\ge2(h_a+qk_a).
\]
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:projected-offsets} with $\lambda=q$.
The common binary projected offset is $L=h_a+qk_a$, and the common
ternary projected offset is $N=h_f+qk_f=h_f$. The dynamic comparison
gives $L\ge N$, while the last display gives $N\ge2L$.
Their nonnegativity forces $L=N=0$. Since $q>0$, every projected
offset $h_s+qk_s=0$ forces $k_s=0$, contradicting positive total $k$.

The cases exhaust all nonnegative secondary slopes. Thus every weak
normalized upper model lies in a case covered by
Lemma~\ref{lem:upper-degenerate}, which supplies all eleven first-offset
equalities.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Reversal after boundary normalization}
For a reversed upper model, normalize the boundaries as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reversed-upper-normal}
\widehat D(x,y)=(x+\lambda y,0),\qquad
\widehat C(x,y)=(x,\sigma),\qquad
\lambda=\frac{D_{01}}{D_{00}},\quad \sigma=(v_c)_1.
\end{equation}
The denominator is positive because the original boundary is
admissible. Each dynamic rule has common outer symbol $d$, so its
first-row matrix comparison and first-offset gap are divided by
$D_{00}$. Each of the other three boundary rules has common inner
symbol $c$. Every digit matrix fixes the first coordinate vector, so
the replacement of $C$ makes these matrix comparisons equalities.
The first coordinate of $v_c$ cancels from their first-offset
differences, and its second coordinate is retained as $\sigma$.
Their first-offset gaps are therefore unchanged. The six swap
comparisons are unchanged as well. This proves preservation of all
weak rules and transfer of all eleven zero gaps back to the original
boundaries.

For a digit map, its homogeneous matrix and its transformed matrix are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-reversal-matrices}
\mathcal H_s=
\begin{pmatrix}1&q_s&h_s\\0&z_s&k_s\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Phi(\mathcal H_s)=J\mathcal H_s^{\mathsf T}J=
\begin{pmatrix}1&k_s&h_s\\0&z_s&q_s\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $J$ reverses the three homogeneous coordinates. Thus $\Phi$
exchanges $q_s$ and $k_s$, preserves $z_s$ and $h_s$, and again gives a
nonnegative affine map with first diagonal entry one. The normalized
boundary images can also be written explicitly:
\[
\Phi(\mathcal H_{\widehat C})
 =\begin{pmatrix}1&\sigma&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Phi(\mathcal H_{\widehat D})
 =\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&\lambda\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
These are the forward normalized forms $C'(x,y)=(x+\sigma y,0)$ and
$D'(x,y)=(x,\lambda)$, respectively.

Since $J^2=I$, the identity
$\Phi(H_1H_2)=\Phi(H_2)\Phi(H_1)$ reverses every interpreted word.
Transposition and the coordinate permutation preserve entrywise order,
and the matrix entry $(0,2)$ is fixed by $\Phi$. This entry is precisely
the first affine offset. Consequently every weak reversed rule becomes
the corresponding weak forward rule, with the same first-offset gap.
The boundary \emph{forms} exchange; the symbol labels do not.
Proposition~\ref{prop:upper} therefore gives zero gaps for the reversed
normalized model too. The normalization is essential here: it ensures
that both transformed boundary matrices still represent affine maps.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Lemma~\ref{lem:matrix} puts the five digit matrices in one common
triangular orientation, with unit first diagonal entries. For lower
digits, Proposition~\ref{prop:lower} applies to the original admissible
boundaries. For upper digits in the forward orientation, normalize the
boundaries and apply Proposition~\ref{prop:upper}. For upper digits in
the reversed orientation, use \eqref{eq:reversed-upper-normal} and
$\Phi$ first, then apply the same proposition. In each case the
normalization transfers all eleven zero first-offset gaps to the
original interpretation. This is the conclusion of the theorem; it
does not assert that every coefficient of every weak rule difference
is zero.
\end{proof}

\section{The unrestricted reversed TOP obstruction}
\label{sec:reversed-top}

We now prove Theorem~\ref{thm:reversed-top}. Throughout this section,
all coefficients are nonnegative real numbers, but no diagonal entry
is assumed to be at least one. We distinguish the digit matrices
$A,B,E,F,G$ from their offsets $a,b,e,f,g$, and write
$\mathcal A(x)=Ax+a$, $\mathcal B(x)=Bx+b$, and similarly for the
ternary digits. Put $\gamma=v_c$. Fix an output coordinate $i$ of
$F_d$, and let $r$ be row $i$ of $M_d$. The two gaps in this coordinate are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-gaps}
ra,\qquad r(b-g).
\end{equation}
Here and below rows act on columns by juxtaposition. Both expressions
are nonnegative by weak orientation. It suffices to derive a
contradiction when at least one is positive. We also write $q=rB$.

For reference, the six reversed swap comparisons have matrix parts
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-swaps}
AE\le EA,\quad BE\le FA,\quad AF\le GA,\quad
BF\le EB,\quad AG\le FB,\quad BG\le GB.
\end{equation}
Their offset parts are obtained by composing the corresponding
affine maps. The boundary and readout comparisons used below are
\begin{align}
r&\le rA,& rG&\le rB,& rg&\le rb,\label{eq:top-readout}\\
B\gamma+b&\le E\gamma+e,\label{eq:top-ec}\\
A^2\gamma+Aa+a&\le F\gamma+f,\label{eq:top-fc}\\
BA\gamma+Ba+b&\le G\gamma+g.\label{eq:top-gc}
\end{align}
All vector and matrix comparisons in this section are entrywise.

\subsection{Arithmetic growth and two readout consequences}

Define the binary interpretation $z(n)$, for positive integers $n$, by
\[
z(1)=\gamma,\qquad z(2n)=\mathcal A(z(n)),\qquad
z(2n+1)=\mathcal B(z(n))\quad(n\ge1).
\]
The reversed swaps and the three $c$-boundary rules give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-conversion}
z(3n)\le\mathcal E(z(n)),\qquad
z(3n+1)\le\mathcal F(z(n)),\qquad
z(3n+2)\le\mathcal G(z(n))\quad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}
To see the induction explicitly, the case $n=1$ consists of
\eqref{eq:top-ec}--\eqref{eq:top-gc}. For $n=2m$, the three
binary recursions, followed by the induction hypothesis, give
$\mathcal A\mathcal E$, $\mathcal B\mathcal E$,
and $\mathcal A\mathcal F$; the first three reversed swaps bound
these by $\mathcal E\mathcal A$, $\mathcal F\mathcal A$,
and $\mathcal G\mathcal A$. For $n=2m+1$, the last three swaps
similarly replace $\mathcal B\mathcal F$, $\mathcal A\mathcal G$,
and $\mathcal B\mathcal G$ by
$\mathcal E\mathcal B$, $\mathcal F\mathcal B$,
and $\mathcal G\mathcal B$. Nonnegativity makes every digit map
monotone, so these substitutions preserve the comparisons.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:top-observed-growth}
If one of the gaps in \eqref{eq:top-gaps} is positive and $q=rB$,
then $q\mathcal A^n(\gamma)$ tends to infinity as $n$ tends to infinity.
Moreover, $qA$ is not a nonnegative scalar multiple of $q$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $R(n)=rz(n)+(v_d)_i$, and choose a positive $\delta$ no larger
than one positive gap. Equations~\eqref{eq:top-readout} and
\eqref{eq:top-conversion} imply
\[
R(n)\le R(2n),\qquad R(3n+2)\le R(2n+1).
\]
One of these two families improves by at least $\delta$ for every
$n\ge1$. Combining one even step and one odd step gives
\[
R(2^e3t+1)+\delta\le R(2^{e+2}t+1)
\qquad(e\ge0,\ t\ge1).
\]
Iteration, using $R\ge0$, yields
$R(2^et+1)\ge k\delta$ whenever $e\ge2k$.
Since $z(2^{n+1}+1)=\mathcal B\mathcal A^n(\gamma)$, subtraction
of the fixed number $rb+(v_d)_i$ proves the claimed divergence.

Suppose next that $qA=\theta q$ with $\theta\ge0$. If $\theta<1$,
the scalar recurrence
\[
q\mathcal A^{n+1}(\gamma)=\theta q\mathcal A^n(\gamma)+qa
\]
is bounded by $q\gamma+qa/(1-\theta)$, a contradiction.
If $\theta\ge1$, then $rG\le q\le qA$. Apply $r$ to
\eqref{eq:top-gc} and use $\gamma,a\ge0$ and $rg\le rb$.
Every nonnegative difference must vanish, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-row-zero}
rg=rb,\qquad qa=0.
\end{equation}
We record the short remaining argument because it will also be useful
below. The matrix and offset parts of $ga\to af$ and $fa\to be$,
together with \eqref{eq:top-readout} and \eqref{eq:top-row-zero}, give
\[
rGa=0,\qquad ra\le rFa,\qquad
rF\le rAF\le rGA\le rBA.
\]
Consequently $0\le ra\le qAa=\theta qa=0$. Both gaps vanish,
contrary to the assumption.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:top-mixed-growth}
Assume a hypothetical nonnegative affine interpretation satisfies all
eleven reversed weak comparisons and has a positive gap in
\eqref{eq:top-gaps}. Let $W$ be any word in its five digit maps and
let $M_W$ be its matrix. Then
\[
rM_W\mathcal B^n(\gamma)\longrightarrow\infty.
\]
In particular, $rM_W\ne0$, and hence $M_W\ne0$, for every such word.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use the same function $R$ and positive gap $\delta$ as in the preceding
proof.
Associate the integer maps $2n,2n+1,3n,3n+1,3n+2$ to
$a,b,e,f,g$, respectively. Their composition for $W$ is $sn+h$,
where $s\ge1$ and $0\le h<s$. Binary recursion and
\eqref{eq:top-conversion} imply
\[
z(sn+h)\le W(z(n)).
\]
Since $z(2^{n+1}-1)=\mathcal B^n(\gamma)$, it is enough to show
that $R(s2^{n+1}-d)$ tends to infinity for the fixed positive integer
$d=s-h$; subtracting the fixed offset of $F_d\circ W$ then gives
the assertion about $rM_W$.

Here is an arithmetic proof of this assertion which uses only the two
rank comparisons in Lemma~\ref{lem:top-observed-growth}.
Consider the accelerated integer map
\[
T(m)=\begin{cases}m/2,&m\text{ even},\\(3m+1)/2,&m\text{ odd},\end{cases}
\]
and stop a positive trajectory at $1$.
At every step above $1$, $R$ does not increase; it decreases by at
least $\delta$ on every even step or on every odd step, according to
which dynamic gap is positive.

Under these hypothetical certificate inequalities, a positive even gap
would force every positive trajectory to reach $1$.
Indeed, an infinite trajectory with infinitely many even steps would
make $R$ negative. An infinite final run of odd steps is also
impossible: after $j$ consecutive odd steps,
$T^j(m)+1=3^j(m+1)/2^j$, so $m+1$ would be divisible by arbitrarily
large powers of two. If a terminating trajectory from $m$ has $k$
even steps, reversing it shows $m\le2^k$: a reversed even step doubles
its argument, and a reversed odd step decreases it. Therefore
$R(m)\ge k\delta$ and tends to infinity with $m$.

If the odd gap is positive, start the same arithmetic map at the
negative integer $-d$. The trajectory stays negative. It has
infinitely many odd steps, since a final run consisting only of
halvings would require a nonzero integer to be divisible by every
power of two. Fix a finite segment with at least $k$ odd steps.
For every sufficiently large $e$, the positive trajectory from
$s2^e-d$ follows exactly the same parity pattern over this segment
and remains above $1$. More explicitly, at step $j$ the difference
between the positive and negative trajectories is
$s3^{o_j}2^{e-j}$, where $o_j$ counts the preceding odd steps.
Taking $e$ larger than the segment length preserves parity, and taking
it still larger keeps all these positive states above $1$.
Hence $R(s2^e-d)\ge k\delta$ for all sufficiently large $e$.
Since $k$ was arbitrary, the required divergence follows.
\end{proof}

The sum of the six nonnegative differences in \eqref{eq:top-swaps}
has trace zero, by cyclicity of the trace. Every diagonal entry of
every one of those differences is therefore zero. We use these six
diagonal equalities even when the full swaps are not exact.

\begin{lemma}[The binary products cannot be ordered]\label{lem:top-ordered}
Under the strict-gap assumption, $AB\not\le BA$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $AB\le BA$. The readout inequalities give
\[
rG\le q=rB\le rAB\le rBA=qA.
\]
Applying $r$ to \eqref{eq:top-gc}, each term in
\[
(qA-rG)\gamma+qa+(rb-rg)\le0
\]
is nonnegative. Thus $qa=0$ and $rb=rg$.
The row $q$ itself satisfies the readout conditions:
$q\le qA$, $qG\le qB$, and $qg\le qb$.
Indeed, $BG\le GB$ gives $qG\le rGB\le qB$, while its offset
comparison $Bg+b\le Gb+g$ gives
$qg\le rGb+rg-rb\le qb$.
Repeating the preceding argument with $q$ in place of $r$ gives
$qBa=0$. As in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:top-observed-growth},
the $ga,fa$ comparisons also give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-ordered-offset-bounds}
ra\le rFa\le qAa.
\end{equation}
Since $rb=rg$, strictness requires $ra>0$.

Choose a coordinate $i$ with $r_i a_i>0$, and let $j$ be the other
coordinate. The equality $qa=0$ gives $q_i=0$.
The row $q$ is nonzero by Lemma~\ref{lem:top-observed-growth},
so $q_j>0$ and $a_j=0$.
From $q_i=(rB)_i=0$ and $qBa=0$, respectively, we obtain
$B_{ii}=B_{ji}=0$. The positive rightmost expression in
\eqref{eq:top-ordered-offset-bounds} gives $A_{ji}>0$.
The $(j,j)$ entry of $AB\le BA$ then gives $B_{ij}=0$.
Consequently $q_j=r_jB_{jj}>0$, so $r_j,B_{jj}>0$.
The $(i,j)$ entry of the same comparison gives $A_{ij}=0$,
and $r\le rA$ in coordinate $j$ gives $A_{jj}\ge1$.
In the coordinate order $(i,j)$, write
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}u&0\\v&t\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&s\end{pmatrix},
\qquad v>0,\quad t\ge1,\quad s>0,
\qquad a=(a_i,0)^{\mathsf T},\ a_i>0.
\]

We now spell out the swap entries that determine the remaining shape.
The diagonal equality $(FA)_{ii}=(BE)_{ii}=0$ gives
$F_{ij}=0$ and $F_{ii}u=0$.
The $(j,i)$ comparison $BF\le EB$ gives $F_{ji}=0$.
The same entry of $AG\le FB$ gives $G_{ii}=G_{ji}=0$;
then $(GA)_{ii}=(AF)_{ii}=F_{ii}u=0$ gives $G_{ij}=0$.
The equality $(EA)_{ii}=(AE)_{ii}$ gives $E_{ij}=0$.
The diagonal equalities for $EB,BF$ and for $GA,AF$ now imply
\[
E_{jj}=F_{jj}=G_{jj}=:\nu.
\]
Since $r_i,a_i>0$ and $F_{ji}=0$, the first inequality in
\eqref{eq:top-ordered-offset-bounds} gives $F_{ii}\ge1$.
Thus $u=0$. The $(j,i)$ comparison $AF\le GA$ gives
$vF_{ii}\le\nu v$, so $\nu>0$.
Finally $(FA)_{jj}=(BE)_{jj}$ gives $\nu t=s\nu$.
Hence $s=t=:\lambda\ge1$, and $rG\le rB$ gives $\nu\le\lambda$.
The resulting shapes are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-ordered-shapes}
\begin{gathered}
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\v&\lambda\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix},\quad
E=\begin{pmatrix}\xi&0\\\eta&\nu\end{pmatrix},\\
F=\begin{pmatrix}\rho&0\\0&\nu\end{pmatrix},\quad
G=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&\nu\end{pmatrix},\qquad
v>0,\quad 1\le\rho\le\nu\le\lambda.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:top-gc} now gives
\[
b_i\le g_i,\qquad
\lambda v\gamma_i+(\lambda^2-\nu)\gamma_j+b_j\le g_j.
\]
Both extra terms are nonnegative. Since $r_i,r_j>0$ and $rb=rg$,
we obtain $g=b$ and $\gamma_i=0$.
The coordinate-$j$ offset comparisons for $fa$ and $ga$ give
\[
\lambda e_j+b_j\le f_j,\qquad vf_i+\lambda f_j\le b_j.
\]
Because $\lambda\ge1$, these imply $f_j=b_j$ and $e_j=0$.
The $ec$ comparison, using $\gamma_i=e_j=0$, becomes
\[
\lambda\gamma_j+b_j\le\nu\gamma_j,
\]
so $b_j=f_j=g_j=0$.
The coordinate-$j$ comparison for $fb\to ag$ consequently gives
$vg_i\le0$, hence $g_i=0$.
Finally the coordinate-$i$ comparison for $ga\to af$ says
$a_i\le g_i=0$, contradicting $a_i>0$.
\end{proof}



\subsection{Excluding support in both directions}

Put $H=A+B+E+F+G$. In two coordinates, every coordinate can return
to every other through a product of digit matrices exactly when
$H_{01},H_{10}>0$. Indeed, these entries themselves give the two
directions; if either entry of $H$ is zero, the same entry is zero
in every power of $H$. We show that the case
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-returns}
H_{01}>0,\qquad H_{10}>0
\end{equation}
is incompatible with a strict dynamic gap.

First all six matrix swaps are exact in this case. Let
$S_0=A+B$, $T_0=E+F+G$, and
$K=T_0S_0-S_0T_0\ge0$, the sum of the six swap differences.
Its diagonal entries are zero. Cyclicity of the trace gives
$\tr(HK)=0$, since $H=S_0+T_0$. But
\[
\tr(HK)=H_{10}K_{01}+H_{01}K_{10}.
\]
Under \eqref{eq:top-returns}, both remaining entries of $K$ vanish.
Every nonnegative summand in $K$ therefore vanishes, proving exactness.
Also $AB\ne BA$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:top-ordered}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:top-middle-invertible}
Under \eqref{eq:top-returns} and the strict-gap assumption, $F$ is
invertible.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $\det F=0$. Lemma~\ref{lem:top-mixed-growth} gives $F^2\ne0$,
so Cayley--Hamilton gives
\[
\tau=\tr F>0,\qquad F^2=\tau F.
\]
Exact swaps also give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-middle-intertwiners}
F(AB)=B^2F,\qquad F(BA)=A^2F.
\end{equation}

Assume first that $\tr B>0$. From the first identity,
$F(B^2F)=\tau B^2F$. Hence $B^2$ preserves the one-dimensional
image of $F$, which is the $\tau$-eigenspace of $F$.
Cayley--Hamilton for $B$, with $\tr B\ne0$, shows that $B$ also
preserves this line. Thus $BF=b_*F$ for a scalar $b_*$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:top-mixed-growth} gives $BF\ne0$, so $b_*\ne0$.
Since $EB=BF$, multiplying on the right by $F$ and cancelling $b_*$
gives $EF=\tau F$.

If $E$ is nonscalar, its centralizer in dimension two consists of
$xI+yE$, as proved in Section~3. Since $EA=AE$, we get
$A=xI+yE$ and therefore $AF=(x+y\tau)F$.
If $E$ is scalar, $EF=\tau F$ makes $E=\tau I$ and
$EB=BF$ gives $B=(b_*/\tau)F$. The equality $GB=BG$ then gives
$GF=FG$, while $AG=FB=b_*F$ and $GA=AF$ imply
\[
b_*GF=G(AG)=(GA)G=A(FG)=(AG)F=b_*\tau F.
\]
Thus $GF=\tau F$, and $\tau AF=A(GF)=(AG)F=b_*\tau F$.
In either case there is a scalar $a_*$ with $AF=a_*F$.

Put $J=AB-BA$. These two scalar actions give $JF=0$ and
$\tr J=0$. The polarized Cayley--Hamilton identity gives
$FJ=\tau J$, while \eqref{eq:top-middle-intertwiners} gives
$FJ=(b_*^2-a_*^2)F$. Taking traces yields
$(b_*^2-a_*^2)\tau=0$; consequently $J=0$.
This contradicts Lemma~\ref{lem:top-ordered}.
If $\tr B=0$ but $\tr A>0$, interchange $A,B$ and $E,G$
in this argument.

Finally suppose both binary traces are zero. Nonnegativity and
$A^2,B^2\ne0$ give
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&a_*\\b_*&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}0&c_*\\d_*&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad a_*,b_*,c_*,d_*>0.
\]
The commuting endpoint swaps force
$E_{00}=E_{11}=e_*$ and $G_{00}=G_{11}=g_*$.
The off-diagonal entries of $FA=BE$ and $GA=AF$ give
\[
F_{00}=(c_*/a_*)e_*,\qquad
F_{11}=(d_*/b_*)e_*,\qquad F_{00}=F_{11}=g_*.
\]
Since $\tr F>0$, $e_*>0$, so $a_*d_*=b_*c_*$ and again
$AB=BA$. This completes all trace cases.
\end{proof}

We next exclude singular binary matrices. Determinants in the exact
swaps give $\det A=\det B$ because $F$ is invertible.
Suppose this common determinant is zero. The nonzero products
$A^2,B^2$ force positive traces. Taking traces in
\eqref{eq:top-middle-intertwiners}, after conjugation by $F$, gives
\[
\tr A=\tr B=\alpha>0,\qquad \tr(AB)=\alpha^2.
\]
For $N=B-A\ne0$, we obtain
$\tr N=\det N=\tr(AN)=0$. The nilpotent-line argument from
Lemma~\ref{lem:matrix-singular-binary} gives
\[
AN=sN,\qquad NA=(\alpha-s)N,\qquad s\in\{0,\alpha\}.
\]
If $s=0$, then $BA=\alpha B$, so $qA=\alpha q$, contrary
to Lemma~\ref{lem:top-observed-growth}.
If $s=\alpha$, the remaining alternative is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-projection-shape}
\begin{gathered}
A^2=BA=\alpha A,\qquad AB=B^2=\alpha B,\\
F=\beta I,\qquad E=(\beta/\alpha)A,\qquad
G=(\beta/\alpha)B,\qquad \alpha,\beta>0.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Here is why the scalar $F$ and the endpoint formulas follow without
assuming $\tr F>0$ in advance. The two intertwining identities now
say that $F$ commutes with both $A$ and $B$. Since $A$ is nonscalar,
write $F=xI+yA$; commutation with $B$ and $AB\ne BA$ force $y=0$.
Invertibility and nonnegativity give $x=\beta>0$.
Writing $E=x'I+y'A$ and using $FA=BE$ determines
$E=(\beta/\alpha)A$; the same argument gives $G$.

The off-diagonal entries of $H$ are positive multiples of those of
$A+B$. Thus $u=(A+B)(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$ is strictly positive.
Equation~\eqref{eq:top-projection-shape} gives
$Au=Bu=\alpha u$ and $Gu=\beta u$.
The nonzero row $r$ satisfies $ru>0$; dotting the readout comparisons
with $u$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-projection-bounds}
1\le\alpha,\qquad 0<\beta\le\alpha.
\end{equation}

We give the offset elimination for this projection case explicitly.
If $\alpha>1$, put $p=rA$ and $s=rB$, and abbreviate
\[
\begin{gathered}
x=p\gamma,\ y=s\gamma,\ u_0=pa,\ u_1=sa,\\
b_0=pb,\ b_1=sb,\ g_0=pg,\ g_1=sg,\ e_0=pe,\ f_0=pf.
\end{gathered}
\]
These symbols denote scalar projections only within this paragraph.
The offset comparisons for $ea,ec,gc,fc,ga,fb,gb$, projected with
$p,p,s,p,p,p,s$, respectively, give
\begin{align*}
\alpha e_0+u_0&\le\beta u_0+e_0,&
\alpha y+b_0&\le\beta x+e_0,\\
\alpha^2x+\alpha u_1+b_1&\le\beta y+g_1,&
\alpha^2x+(\alpha+1)u_0&\le\beta x+f_0,\\
\alpha f_0+u_0&\le\beta u_1+g_0,&
\alpha g_0+u_0&\le\beta b_0+f_0,\\
\alpha g_1+b_1&\le\beta b_1+g_1.
\end{align*}
The first and last imply $e_0\le u_0$ and $g_1\le b_1$.
Replace $e_0$ by $u_0$ in the second inequality, then add the second,
third, fifth, sixth, and fourth inequalities with respective weights
$\beta,\alpha,\alpha,1,\alpha^2-1$. Cancellation gives
\begin{align*}
&\bigl[(\alpha^2-1)(\alpha^2-\beta)
       +(\alpha^3-\beta^2)\bigr]x
 +\bigl[\alpha^2(\alpha+1)-\beta\bigr]u_0\\
&\hspace{35mm}+\alpha(\alpha-\beta)u_1
 +\alpha(b_1-g_1)\le0.
\end{align*}
Every term is nonnegative, and the coefficients of $x,u_0$ are
strictly positive by \eqref{eq:top-projection-bounds}.
Thus $x=u_0=0$, and $\alpha y+b_0\le\beta x+u_0$ gives $b_0=0$.
Since $r\le p$, we obtain $ra=rb=0$, and then $rg=0$.

If $\alpha=1$, positivity of $u$ and $r\le rA$ imply $rA=r$:
the nonnegative row $rA-r$ has zero product with $u>0$.
With $q=rB$, the projection identities give
\[
qA=r,\quad qB=q,\quad rF=\beta r,\quad
rG=\beta q,\quad qE=\beta r.
\]
Project the offset comparisons for $fa,ga,gc,ec$ by $r,r,r,q$
and give them weights $\beta,\beta,1,\beta$.
After cancellation their sum is
\[
\beta(1-\beta)ra+(1-\beta^2)qa+(1-\beta^2)r\gamma
 +(1+\beta)(rb-rg)+\beta qb\le0.
\]
All terms are nonnegative. Since $\beta>0$, $qb=0$.
Together with $qB=q$, this makes
$q\mathcal B^n(\gamma)=q\gamma$ for every $n$, contradicting
Lemma~\ref{lem:top-mixed-growth} with the one-letter prefix $b$.
Thus the singular-binary case is impossible.

We have proved that $A,B,F$ are invertible. The exact swaps then
make $E,G$ invertible as well. We check the trace hypotheses needed
to reuse the nonsingular classification from Section~3.
Equation~\eqref{eq:top-middle-intertwiners} gives
$\tr A=\tr B\ge0$ as before. If both traces were zero,
Cayley--Hamilton and $\det A=\det B$ would give $A^2=B^2$;
cancellation of $F$ in \eqref{eq:top-middle-intertwiners} would then
give $AB=BA$. Hence both binary traces are positive.
The ternary matrices have the same trace because $BF=EB$ and
$AF=GA$ are invertible intertwinings. If that trace were zero,
nonnegativity would make all their diagonal entries zero.
The equality $BG=GB$, with $G$ invertible, would force
$B_{00}=B_{11}>0$.
The off-diagonal entries of $BF=EB$ would then give $E=F$;
$FA=BE$ and $EA=AE$ would give $FA=BF=AF$, and cancellation
would force $A=B$. This again contradicts
Lemma~\ref{lem:top-ordered}. Thus the ternary traces are positive.

Transpose all five matrices and apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:matrix-nonsingular-shape}. Transposing back gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-nonsingular-shape}
\begin{gathered}
\mu,t>0,\qquad
\tr A=\tr B=3\mu,\qquad\det A=\det B=2\mu^2,\\
A(B-A)=2\mu(B-A),\qquad (B-A)A=\mu(B-A),\\
E=t(2A-\mu I),\quad F=t(A+B-\mu I),\quad G=t(2B-\mu I).
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Put $U=A-\mu I$, $V=B-\mu I$, and $S=U+V$.
The trace and determinant equations make $U,V$ nonnegative, by the
same diagonal calculation as in Section~3. The product identities
are now the transposes of \eqref{eq:matrix-shifted-products}:
\[
U^2=VU=\mu U,\qquad UV=V^2=\mu V,
\qquad US=VS=\mu S,\qquad S^2=2\mu S.
\]
Since $H_{ij}=(1+3t)S_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$, both off-diagonal
entries of $S$ are positive. Therefore
\[
u=S(1,1)^{\mathsf T}>0,\qquad
Au=Bu=\lambda u,\qquad Eu=Fu=Gu=\nu u,
\quad \lambda=2\mu,\quad\nu=3t\mu.
\]
Dotting \eqref{eq:top-readout} with $u$ gives
$1\le\lambda$ and $0<\nu\le\lambda$.

The case $\lambda=1$ needs a separate argument.
The positive row $w=(1,1)S$ satisfies
$w(A+B)=4\mu w$ and $w(E+F+G)=9t\mu w$.
Summing the six offset comparisons gives
\[
3(a+b)+(A+B)(e+f+g)
 \le(E+F+G)(a+b)+2(e+f+g).
\]
When $\lambda=1$, $\mu=1/2$, and projection by $w$ reduces this to
$3(1-\nu)w(a+b)\le0$. If $\nu<1$, positivity of $w$ implies
$a=b=0$, so neither gap is positive.
If $\nu=1$, then $t=2/3$. The positive vector $u$ and
$r\le rA$ give $rA=r$; likewise $rG\le rB$ and their equal
products with $u$ give $rG=rB$. Substitution of
$G=(2/3)(2B-I/2)$ gives $rB=r$.
Thus $qA=q$, contrary to Lemma~\ref{lem:top-observed-growth}.

It remains to exclude $\lambda>1$ in this support case.
All diagonal entries of $A,B$ are at least $\mu>0$, and
both off-diagonal entries of $A+B$ are positive.
At least one of $a,b$ is nonzero. Apply its digit to $\gamma$,
obtaining a nonzero nonnegative vector, then apply a binary digit
whose positive off-diagonal transfers any missing coordinate.
Its positive diagonal preserves the already positive coordinate.
Thus some $p_0\in\{4,5,6,7\}$ has $z(p_0)>0$ coordinatewise,
and hence $z(p_0)\ge cu$ for a number $c>0$.
Appending $k$ zero digits yields
$z(p_0 2^k)\ge c\lambda^ku$.

Choose $K,M\ge0$ with $\gamma\le Ku$ and $e,f,g\le Mu$.
Because all three ternary matrices send $u$ to
$\nu u\le\lambda u$, the conversion induction gives
\[
z(m)\le(K+jM)\lambda^ju\qquad(3^j\le m<2\cdot3^j).
\]
For each $n$, choose $k$ with
$3^{2(n+1)}\le p_0 2^k<2\cdot3^{2(n+1)}$.
Taking the least possible $k$ ensures the upper bound, and
$p_0\le7$ gives $k\ge3n$ as in the elementary comparison below
\eqref{eq:top-ternary-upper}. Since $u>0$, comparison of the two
vector bounds gives
\[
c\lambda^{3n}\le\bigl(K+2(n+1)M\bigr)\lambda^{2(n+1)},
\quad\text{hence}\quad
c\lambda^n\le\lambda^2\bigl(K+2(n+1)M\bigr).
\]
This is impossible for $\lambda>1$, since an exponential cannot be
bounded by a linear function. Thus \eqref{eq:top-returns} is excluded.

Consequently $H_{01}=0$ or $H_{10}=0$. Since every digit matrix is
nonnegative, all five share the corresponding zero entry. This proves
the required common triangular orientation.

\subsection{The active triangular coordinate}

It remains to consider a common triangular orientation of the five
digit matrices. We may take it to be upper triangular. This coordinate
choice requires some care because the output coordinate of $F_d$
has already been fixed. If the common orientation is lower triangular,
put $P=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$
and replace $F_s$ by $P\circ F_s\circ P$ for
$s\in\{a,b,c,e,f,g\}$, but replace $F_d$ by $F_d\circ P$.
Thus $M_d$ changes to $M_dP$ and $v_d$ stays unchanged.
Every reversed word comparison is preserved; the two offset vectors
$v_{da}-v_d$ and $v_{db}-v_{dg}$ are unchanged. In particular, the
chosen strict output coordinate is preserved. This is an input
coordinate change at $d$, not a permutation of its output.

Write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-upper-shapes}
A=\begin{pmatrix}x&p\\0&\alpha\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}y&p_b\\0&\beta\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
As before, $q=rB$ denotes the readout row.
Lemma~\ref{lem:top-observed-growth} shows that $q_0>0$: if $q_0=0$,
then $qA=\alpha q$. Hence $r_0>0$ and $y>0$.
The first coordinates of $r\le rA$ and $rG\le rB$ now give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-active-bounds}
x\ge1,\qquad y>0,\qquad G_{00}\le y.
\end{equation}
Lemma~\ref{lem:top-ordered} also gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-cross-decrease}
(BA)_{01}<(AB)_{01}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the diagonal entries of $AB$ and $BA$ agree, and their lower
left entries are zero.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:top-contracting-secondary}
Under the strict-gap assumption, $\alpha<1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $\alpha\ge1$, then $I\le A$. Thus $q\le qA$, and the proof of
\eqref{eq:top-row-zero} again gives $rg=rb$ and $qa=0$.
Since $q_0>0$, we have $a_0=0$.
If $r_1=0$, this already gives $ra=0$. Otherwise, if $\beta>0$,
then $q_1=r_0p_b+r_1\beta>0$, and $qa=0$ gives $a_1=0$.
If $\beta=0$, \eqref{eq:top-cross-decrease} reads
$yp+\alpha p_b<xp_b$ and forces $p_b>0$; again
$q_1>0$ and $a_1=0$. Both gaps vanish in every case.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A zero active ternary diagonal}

We first dispose of $F_{00}=0$. The $(0,0)$ entries of
$BE\le FA$ and $AG\le FB$, with \eqref{eq:top-active-bounds},
force $E_{00}=G_{00}=0$. Put $u=E_{01}$, $v=F_{01}$,
$w=G_{01}$ and $\epsilon=E_{11}$, $\phi=F_{11}$,
$\psi=G_{11}$. The relevant upper right comparisons are
\begin{align*}
xu+p\epsilon&\le\alpha u,&
yu+p_b\epsilon&\le\alpha v,&
xv+p\phi&\le\alpha w,\\
yv+p_b\phi&\le\beta u,&
xw+p\psi&\le\beta v,&
yw+p_b\psi&\le\beta w.
\end{align*}
If $v>0$, the fourth comparison forces $u>0$ and $\beta>0$.
The first then gives $\alpha\ge x\ge1$, contradicting
Lemma~\ref{lem:top-contracting-secondary}. Thus $v=0$.
The second and fifth comparisons imply $u=w=0$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:top-cross-decrease} implies $p+p_b>0$;
the third and fourth comparisons therefore give $\phi=0$,
and the fifth and sixth give $\psi=0$. Hence $F=G=0$.

The offset parts of $fa\to be$ and $ga\to af$ now read
$Be+b\le f$ and $Af+a\le g$. After applying $r$ and using
$r\le rA$ and $rg\le rb$, we obtain
\[
rb\le rBe+rb\le rf\le rAf\le rAf+ra\le rg\le rb.
\]
All comparisons are equalities. In particular, $ra=0$ and $rb=rg$,
which excludes the zero active ternary diagonal.

\subsection{Positive active diagonals and the unit cases}

Suppose $F_{00}>0$. The diagonal equalities in the six swaps give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-lambda-nu}
A_{00}=B_{00}=\lambda\ge1,\qquad
E_{00}=F_{00}=G_{00}=\nu,\qquad 0<\nu\le\lambda.
\end{equation}
For completeness, $BF$ and $EB$ have equal $(0,0)$ entries,
so $E_{00}=F_{00}$ because $B_{00}>0$.
Equality for $AF$ and $GA$ gives $G_{00}=F_{00}$; equality
for $BE$ and $FA$ then gives $A_{00}=B_{00}$.
The last bound is \eqref{eq:top-active-bounds}.

If $\lambda=\nu=1$, the previously proved admissible obstruction
applies after a change of the two boundary maps. Retain all five digit
maps and replace the boundaries by
\[
\widehat F_c(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}x+\gamma,
\qquad
\widehat F_d(x)=
\begin{pmatrix}1&r_1/r_0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}x.
\]
Every digit matrix $X$ satisfies $X\diag(1,0)=\diag(1,0)$.
The three $c$-boundary matrix comparisons are therefore equalities,
and their offsets are the original offsets. The two $d$-boundary
comparisons follow by dividing the selected original readout row by
$r_0$; their second output coordinates are zero. All seven new
matrices have first diagonal entry one. Thus Theorem~\ref{thm:main},
already proved in Sections~3--6, applies to this reversed admissible
interpretation. Its two dynamic gaps are the original gaps divided
by $r_0>0$, a contradiction.

Suppose instead that $\lambda=1$ and $0<\nu<1$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:top-contracting-secondary} gives $0\le\alpha<1$.
Define the nonnegative row
\[
w=\left(1,\frac{p}{1-\alpha}\right),\qquad wA=w.
\]
From $AE\le EA$, its upper right entry gives
$E_{01}+pE_{11}\le\nu p+\alpha E_{01}$, hence
$wE\le\nu w$. The corresponding offset comparison is
$Ae+a\le Ea+e$. Apply $w$ and cancel $we$ to obtain
\[
0\le wa\le wEa\le\nu wa.
\]
Thus $wa=0$ and $w\mathcal A^n(\gamma)=w\gamma$ for every $n$.
The second coordinate of the same orbit obeys the bounded recurrence
$t_{n+1}=\alpha t_n+a_1$, with
$t_n\le\gamma_1+a_1/(1-\alpha)$.
The first coordinate is at most $w\gamma$ because $w_0=1$.
Consequently the entire orbit, and therefore its value under the
nonnegative row $q=rB$, is bounded. This contradicts
Lemma~\ref{lem:top-observed-growth}.

\subsection{The expanding active diagonal}

Only $\lambda>1$ remains. Keep
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&p\\0&\alpha\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&p_b\\0&\beta\end{pmatrix},
\qquad 0\le\alpha<1.
\]
We first eliminate $\beta>1$, including cases where the other
ternary diagonal entries vanish. Equality of the diagonal swap entries
gives $E_{11}=F_{11}$ and
$\beta E_{11}=\alpha F_{11}$. Hence $E_{11}=F_{11}=0$.
The second-coordinate offset comparisons of $fa\to be$ and
$eb\to bf$ become
\[
\beta e_1+b_1\le f_1,\qquad
\beta f_1+b_1\le e_1.
\]
Since $\beta>1$, they force $e_1=f_1=b_1=0$.
The second coordinate of $ea\to ae$ then gives $a_1=0$,
and \eqref{eq:top-ec} gives $\gamma_1=0$.

The first-coordinate offset comparison of $gb\to bg$ is
\[
\lambda g_0+p_bg_1+b_0\le\nu b_0+g_0.
\]
Since $\nu\le\lambda$ and $\lambda>1$, it implies $g_0\le b_0$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:top-gc} reduces in its first coordinate to
\[
(\lambda^2-\nu)\gamma_0+\lambda a_0+b_0\le g_0.
\]
Both coefficients on the left are positive, so
$\gamma_0=a_0=0$. The first coordinate of $ea\to ae$ then gives
$(\lambda-1)e_0\le0$, so $e_0=0$; finally
\eqref{eq:top-ec} gives $b_0=0$.
We have $a=b=0$, which precludes a positive gap because
$ra=0$ and $0\le r(b-g)=-rg\le0$.

We may therefore assume $0\le\beta\le1$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:top-cross-decrease} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-delta}
\Delta=(\lambda-\alpha)p_b-(\lambda-\beta)p>0.
\end{equation}
In particular $p_b>0$. Write
\[
E=\begin{pmatrix}\nu&u\\0&\epsilon\end{pmatrix},\qquad
F=\begin{pmatrix}\nu&v\\0&\phi\end{pmatrix},\qquad
G=\begin{pmatrix}\nu&w\\0&\psi\end{pmatrix}.
\]
We next show $\epsilon,\phi,\psi\le\nu$. The upper right
comparison for $gb\to bg$ reads
\[
(\lambda-\beta)w+p_b(\psi-\nu)\le0,
\]
so $\psi\le\nu$. If $\epsilon>\nu$, the comparison for
$ea\to ae$,
$(\lambda-\alpha)u+p(\epsilon-\nu)\le0$, forces $u=p=0$.
The comparisons for $fa\to be$ and $eb\to bf$ would then give
$p_b\epsilon\le\alpha v$ and
$\lambda v+p_b\phi\le\nu p_b$. Thus
$\lambda\epsilon\le\alpha\nu$, contradicting
$\epsilon>\nu$ and $\alpha<\lambda$.
Hence $\epsilon\le\nu$.

If $\alpha>0$, equality of the lower diagonal entries of $AF$ and
$GA$ gives $\phi=\psi\le\nu$. If $\alpha=0$ and $\phi>\nu$,
the upper right comparison for $ga\to af$,
$\lambda v+p(\phi-\nu)\le0$, forces $v=p=0$.
The comparison for $ea\to ae$ then gives $u=0$, and that for
$eb\to bf$ gives $p_b\phi\le\nu p_b$, again a contradiction.
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-all-ternary-diagonals}
\epsilon,\phi,\psi\le\nu\le\lambda.
\end{equation}

Although the three upper right entries need not be bounded by
$\lambda$, all three ternary matrices admit a common positive
upper bound in a suitably weighted coordinate system. Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-upper-ray}
L=\frac{3\lambda-1}{2},\qquad
t=\frac{L-\lambda}{1+u+v+w},\qquad h=(1,t)^{\mathsf T}.
\end{equation}
Then $t>0$, $L>\lambda>1$, and
\[
L^2<\lambda^3,
\qquad
\lambda^3-L^2=\frac{(\lambda-1)^2(4\lambda-1)}4.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:top-all-ternary-diagonals} gives
$Eh,Fh,Gh\le Lh$: for the first coordinate, each of
$ut,vt,wt$ is at most $L-\lambda$; for the second coordinate,
each lower diagonal is at most $\lambda<L$.

At least one of $a,b$ is nonzero under the strict-gap assumption.
Starting from $\gamma$, apply that digit once to obtain a nonzero
nonnegative vector. Since $A_{00}=\lambda>0$ and $p+p_b>0$,
one further application of $\mathcal A$ or $\mathcal B$ produces
a positive first coordinate. Thus there is a binary prefix
$p_0\in\{4,5,6,7\}$ with $z(p_0)_0=c>0$.
Appending $k$ binary zero digits gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-binary-lower}
z(p_0 2^k)_0\ge c\lambda^k.
\end{equation}
On the other hand, since $h$ is strictly positive, choose $K,M\ge0$
with $\gamma\le Kh$ and $e,f,g\le Mh$.
Repeated application of \eqref{eq:top-conversion} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:top-ternary-upper}
z(m)\le(K+jM)L^jh\qquad(3^j\le m<2\cdot3^j).
\end{equation}
The induction removes the last ternary digit of $m$.
Its scalar estimate is
$L(K+jM)L^j+M\le(K+(j+1)M)L^{j+1}$, using $L\ge1$.

For each $n\ge0$, choose the least $k$ for which
$3^{2(n+1)}\le p_0 2^k$. Minimality gives
$p_0 2^k<2\cdot3^{2(n+1)}$, and $k\ge3n$ because
\[
p_0 2^{3n}\le7\cdot8^n<9\cdot9^n=3^{2(n+1)}.
\]
Combine \eqref{eq:top-binary-lower} and
\eqref{eq:top-ternary-upper}, using $h_0=1$, to obtain
\[
c\lambda^{3n}\le\bigl(K+2(n+1)M\bigr)L^{2(n+1)}.
\]
This would bound $(\lambda^3/L^2)^n$ by a linear function of $n$,
although $\lambda^3/L^2>1$. The contradiction is elementary:
if $\rho=1+\eta>1$, the binomial estimate
$\rho^n\ge\binom n2\eta^2$ already exceeds every fixed linear
bound for sufficiently large $n$.

\begin{proof}[Conclusion of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:reversed-top}]
If one coordinate of either nonnegative dynamic gap vector were
positive, the preceding argument would first force a common triangular
orientation and would then exclude $F_{00}=0$, $\lambda=\nu=1$,
$\lambda=1>\nu$, and $\lambda>1$. These cases are exhaustive by
\eqref{eq:top-lambda-nu}. Hence every coordinate of both gap vectors
is zero:
$v_{da}=v_d$ and $v_{db}=v_{dg}$.
No positive uniform decrement is therefore available for either
reversed TOP rule in two nonnegative real affine coordinates.
The conclusion concerns these two offset vectors. It does not assert
that all eleven offset gaps vanish without the diagonal assumptions,
and it does not settle the unrestricted forward TOP problem.
\end{proof}


\section{Forward growth and a contraction obstruction}
\label{sec:further-necessary}
\label{sec:forward-necessary}

This section treats a forward case outside the first theorem's diagonal
assumptions. It uses only nine weak comparisons, works in every finite
dimension, and imposes a contraction condition only on the binary
matrix $M_a$. The critical and expanding cases remain open.

Let $F_s(x)=M_sx+v_s$ have nonnegative real matrices and offsets in
$m\ge1$ coordinates, for $s\in\{a,b,c,e,f,g\}$. Assume the following
nine coefficientwise weak comparisons:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forward-nine}
\begin{array}{lll}
 F_{ae}\ge F_{ea},&F_{af}\ge F_{eb},&F_{ag}\ge F_{fa},\\
 F_{be}\ge F_{fb},&F_{bf}\ge F_{ga},&F_{bg}\ge F_{gb},\\
 F_{ce}\ge F_{cb},&F_{cf}\ge F_{caa},&F_{cg}\ge F_{cab}.
\end{array}
\end{equation}
Here $F_{uv}=F_u\circ F_v$, and an inequality compares both the matrix
and the offset. Fix a coordinate $i$, and write
\[
 \rho(x)=(F_c(x))_i=r x+\gamma,
 \qquad r=(M_c)_{i,*},\qquad \gamma=(v_c)_i.
\]
The three nonnegative boundary offset gaps at this coordinate are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forward-boundary-gaps}
 \delta_e=(v_{ce}-v_{cb})_i,\qquad
 \delta_f=(v_{cf}-v_{caa})_i,\qquad
 \delta_g=(v_{cg}-v_{cab})_i.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:forward-five-growth}
Under \eqref{eq:forward-nine}, for every integer $n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forward-five-growth}
 \rho\bigl(F_e^{\,n}(0)\bigr)
 \ge \left\lfloor\frac n5\right\rfloor
       (\delta_e+\delta_f+\delta_g).
\end{equation}
In particular, a positive boundary offset gap makes this observed affine
orbit unbounded.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Identify the binary letters $a,b$ with $0,1$, and assign the values
$0,1,2$ to the carry letters $e,f,g$. For a binary word $w$ of length
$d$, let $\operatorname{val}(w)$ be its usual binary value, with the
leftmost bit most significant, and put
\[
 \operatorname{code}(w)=2^d+\operatorname{val}(w).
\]
Moving a carry through one bit uses the six pairs
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccccc}
 (b,h)&(0,0)&(0,1)&(0,2)&(1,0)&(1,1)&(1,2)\\
 (h',b')&(0,0)&(0,1)&(1,0)&(1,1)&(2,0)&(2,1).
\end{array}
\]
Each pair satisfies $3b+h=2h'+b'$ and is exactly the corresponding
swap in \eqref{eq:forward-nine}. Thus moving the final $e$ in $we$
to the left produces a carry $h$ and a binary word $v$ of the same
length as $w$, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forward-carry-value}
 3\operatorname{val}(w)=h\,2^d+\operatorname{val}(v).
\end{equation}
At the outer boundary, replace $ce,cf,cg$ by $cb,caa,cab$, respectively.
Starting with $w_0$ empty, this defines $w_{k+1}=u_{h_k}v_k$, where
\[
 u_e=b,\qquad u_f=aa,\qquad u_g=ab.
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:forward-carry-value} gives
$\operatorname{code}(w_{k+1})=3\operatorname{code}(w_k)$, hence
$\operatorname{code}(w_k)=3^k$. If $d_k=|w_k|$, then
\[
 2^{d_k}\le3^k<2^{d_k+1},\qquad
 \frac{3+h_k}{3}\le x_k<\frac{4+h_k}{3},
 \qquad x_k=\frac{3^k}{2^{d_k}}\in[1,2),
\]
where $h_k$ in the fractions denotes the numerical carry value.

Every five consecutive stages contain all three carry letters. The
following seventeen cases prove the finite cover: in the group labelled
$h$, the displayed $(j,t)$ satisfies $0\le j<5$, $t\ge0$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forward-cover-target}
 \frac{3+h}{3}<\frac{3^j x}{2^t}<\frac{4+h}{3}.
\end{equation}
All left endpoints are included; right endpoints are excluded except
where a closing square bracket is shown.
\[
\begin{array}[t]{c|rr}
 \multicolumn{3}{c}{h=0}\\
 \text{range of }x&j&t\\ \hline
 {[1,32/27)} & 2 & 3\\
 {[32/27,4/3)} & 0 & 0\\
 {[4/3,128/81)} & 3 & 5\\
 {[128/81,16/9)} & 1 & 2\\
 {[16/9,2]} & 4 & 7
\end{array}
\qquad
\begin{array}[t]{c|rr}
 \multicolumn{3}{c}{h=1}\\
 \text{range of }x&j&t\\ \hline
 {[1,10/9)} & 1 & 1\\
 {[10/9,320/243)} & 4 & 6\\
 {[320/243,40/27)} & 2 & 3\\
 {[40/27,5/3)} & 0 & 0\\
 {[5/3,160/81)} & 3 & 5\\
 {[160/81,2]} & 1 & 2
\end{array}
\qquad
\begin{array}[t]{c|rr}
 \multicolumn{3}{c}{h=2}\\
 \text{range of }x&j&t\\ \hline
 {[1,32/27)} & 3 & 4\\
 {[32/27,4/3)} & 1 & 1\\
 {[4/3,128/81)} & 4 & 6\\
 {[128/81,16/9)} & 2 & 3\\
 {[16/9,2)} & 0 & 0\\
 {\{2\}} & 3 & 5
\end{array}
\]
Each instance of \eqref{eq:forward-cover-target} follows by multiplying
the two endpoints by $3^j/2^t$. Applying the cover to $x_k$ gives
\[
 \frac{3^j x_k}{2^t}=\frac{3^{k+j}}{2^{d_k+t}}\in(1,2).
\]
The unique binary exponent for $3^{k+j}$ is therefore $d_k+t$.
The strict target interval identifies $h_{k+j}=h$. This proves the
five-stage assertion, including all endpoint cases.

The same conversion retains its offset gaps. Nonnegativity makes every
map monotone, and a weak root comparison contributes at least its
offset difference at every nonnegative input. Consequently
\[
 \rho\bigl(F_w(F_e(x))\bigr)
 \ge \rho\bigl(F_{w'}(x)\bigr)+\delta_h
 \qquad(x\ge0),
\]
where $w'$ and $h$ are the word and carry obtained from $we$.
Applying this successively to the $n$ copies of $e$ gives
\[
 \rho\bigl(F_e^{\,n}(0)\bigr)
 \ge \rho\bigl(F_{w_n}(0)\bigr)
       +\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta_{h_k}
 \ge \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta_{h_k}.
\]
Each disjoint block of five terms contains all three nonnegative gaps,
so its sum is at least $\delta_e+\delta_f+\delta_g$. Discarding the
remaining nonnegative terms proves \eqref{eq:forward-five-growth}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:forward-contraction}
Assume \eqref{eq:forward-nine}. If a strictly positive row $w$ and a
number $0\le\lambda<1$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forward-a-contraction}
 wM_a\le\lambda w,
\end{equation}
then $\delta_e=\delta_f=\delta_g=0$ at every coordinate of $F_c$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix the observed coordinate and rescale $w$ so that $w\ge r$.
The inequality $F_{ae}\ge F_{ea}$ and monotonicity imply
\[
 F_e^{\,n}\circ F_a^{\,k}\le F_a^{\,k}\circ F_e^{\,n}
 \quad\text{on the nonnegative orthant}.
\]
Writing $x_n=F_e^{\,n}(0)$ and using $F_a^{\,k}(0)\ge0$ gives
\[
 x_n\le F_e^{\,n}\bigl(F_a^{\,k}(0)\bigr)
     \le F_a^{\,k}(x_n).
\]
Meanwhile \eqref{eq:forward-a-contraction}, iterated through the affine
offset, gives for every $x\ge0$
\[
 wF_a^{\,k}(x)
 \le\lambda^k wx+\frac{wv_a}{1-\lambda}.
\]
It follows that
\[
 rx_n\le\lambda^k wx_n+\frac{wv_a}{1-\lambda}.
\]
For fixed $n$, let $k\to\infty$. Thus
\[
 \rho(x_n)\le\frac{wv_a}{1-\lambda}+\gamma
 \qquad(n\ge0).
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:forward-five-growth} forces the sum of the three
nonnegative gaps, and hence each gap, to vanish. Only $M_a$ is subject
to the contraction condition \eqref{eq:forward-a-contraction}.
\end{proof}

The criterion includes matrices whose column sums are all below one
(take $w=(1,\ldots,1)$), while $(M_a)_{ii}\le\lambda<1$ separates this
regime from the diagonal-admissible class of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

\noindent\textbf{Explicit two-coordinate corollary.}
Under \eqref{eq:forward-nine}, a positive gap among
$\delta_e,\delta_f,\delta_g$ in two coordinates requires
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forward-two-necessary}
 (M_a)_{00}\ge1\quad\text{or}\quad (M_a)_{11}\ge1
 \quad\text{or}\quad
 (1-(M_a)_{00})(1-(M_a)_{11})
 \le (M_a)_{01}(M_a)_{10}.
\end{equation}
\begin{proof}
Suppose all three alternatives fail, and set
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \Delta&=(1-(M_a)_{00})(1-(M_a)_{11})
              -(M_a)_{01}(M_a)_{10}>0,\\
 w&=\bigl(1-(M_a)_{11}+(M_a)_{10},\,
         1-(M_a)_{00}+(M_a)_{01}\bigr).
 \end{aligned}
\]
Both entries of $w$ are positive. Direct multiplication gives
$wM_a=w-\Delta(1,1)$. Put $S=w_0+w_1$ and
$\lambda=1-\Delta/S$. Since $wM_a\ge0$, we have
$0<\Delta\le w_i\le S$ for each $i$, so $0\le\lambda<1$ and
\[
 (wM_a)_i=w_i-\Delta\le(1-\Delta/S)w_i=\lambda w_i.
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:forward-contraction} makes every boundary offset gap
zero, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

Condition \eqref{eq:forward-two-necessary} is a necessary condition
for a positive boundary gap. It is not a sufficiency statement or a
complete exclusion of the nonnegative forward interpretation format.

\section{Finite patterns with equal first hitting times}
\label{sec:finite-patterns}

We now consider finite collections of integer trajectories directly.
Throughout this section, $\mathbb N$ includes zero, and
\[
T(x)=
\begin{cases}
x/2,&x\text{ even},\\
(3x+1)/2,&x\text{ odd}
\end{cases}
\qquad(x\in\mathbb Z_{>0}).
\]
Write $o_k(x)$ for the number of odd integers among
$x,T(x),\ldots,T^{k-1}(x)$, with $o_0(x)=0$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:finite-pattern-hitting}
For every finite set $F\subset\mathbb N$ and every $L\in\mathbb N$,
there are integers $x>\max\{1,L\}$ and $H,R\in\mathbb N$ such that,
for every $n\in F$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-pattern-hitting}
T^H(x+n)=1,\qquad o_H(x+n)=R,\qquad
T^j(x+n)>1\quad(0\le j<H).
\end{equation}
In particular, every prescribed finite pattern has arbitrarily large
translates whose members have the same finite first hitting time of
one and the same number of odd steps before that time.
\end{theorem}

Taking $F=\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-1\}$ gives arbitrarily long intervals
with these properties. For example, the starts $28,29,30$ all
reach $20$ after seven steps and first reach one after thirteen
steps, each having used five odd steps.
Earlier studies of equal-height pairs and consecutive runs include
Garner~\cite{Garner1985} and Gao~\cite{Gao1993};
LaDue~\cite{LaDue2018} studies clusters with equal total stopping times.
This section gives a self-contained construction and its Lean
formalization. We make no claim of historical priority for the
existence statement.
The proof below first synchronizes a finite pattern on an arithmetic
progression, then chooses members whose common endpoint is a power of
two. The latter step uses the classical fact that powers of two exhaust
the units modulo powers of three. This fact also underlies the
back-tracing argument in Monks~\cite[Lemma~3.4]{Monks2006}.

\subsection{Parity classes and prescribed gaps}

We first record the dependence of a finite trajectory on its starting
residue class.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:finite-dyadic-prefix}
For $c\in\mathbb Z_{>0}$, $k,t\in\mathbb N$, and $0\le j\le k$,
\begin{align}
T^j(c+2^kt)
 &=T^j(c)+2^{k-j}3^{o_j(c)}t,
 \label{eq:finite-dyadic-prefix}\\
o_j(c+2^kt)&=o_j(c).
 \label{eq:finite-dyadic-count}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The assertions hold at $j=0$. If $j<k$, the difference in
\eqref{eq:finite-dyadic-prefix} is even, so the two current integers
have the same parity. An even step divides the difference by two;
an odd step multiplies it by $3/2$ and adds one to both odd counts.
This proves the induction.
\end{proof}

In particular, the first $k$ parities are constant on each positive
residue class modulo $2^k$. If a current value is $A+3^rt$, either
parity can be prescribed by restricting $t$ to $\varepsilon+2u$,
where $\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}$. After the prescribed actual step,
the coefficient of $u$ is $3^r$ or $3^{r+1}$ according as that
step is even or odd. Thus this prescription can be repeated, and
each additional step doubles the parameter modulus.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:finite-pair}
For every $n\in\mathbb N$, there are $k\in\mathbb N$ and
$a\in\mathbb Z_{>0}$
such that, for every $t\in\mathbb N$,
\begin{align*}
T^k(a+2^kt)&=T^k(a+2^kt+n),\\
o_k(a+2^kt)&=o_k(a+2^kt+n).
\end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We use strong induction on $n$. For $n=0$, take $k=0$ and $a=1$.
For $n=1$, the two trajectories
\[
\begin{aligned}
4+8t&\longmapsto2+4t\longmapsto1+2t\longmapsto2+3t,\\
5+8t&\longmapsto8+12t\longmapsto4+6t\longmapsto2+3t
\end{aligned}
\]
each have one odd step. This gives $k=3$ and $a=4$.

If $n=2m>0$, use the progression $a+2^kt$ for $m$ supplied
by induction. The starts $2a+2^{k+1}t$ and
$2a+2^{k+1}t+2m$ take one even step to that pair. They therefore
coalesce after $k+1$ steps, with equal odd counts.

It remains to treat odd $n>1$. Choose $m,c,d$ by
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
n\bmod4&m&c&d\\ \hline
1&(3n+1)/4&1&2\\
3&(3n-1)/4&2&1
\end{array}
\]
so that $0<m<n$ and $3n+d=4m+c$.
Let $a+2^kt$ be the progression for $m$.
Because $2^k$ is invertible modulo three, some
$q\in\{0,1,2\}$ makes $4(a+2^kq)-c$ divisible by three.
The integer
\[
a'=\frac{4(a+2^kq)-c}{3}
\]
is positive. For $X=a'+2^{k+2}t$ and
$Y=a+2^k(q+3t)$, we have
\[
3X+c=4Y,\qquad 3(X+n)+d=4(Y+m).
\]
If $3z+1=4w$, then $z\equiv1\pmod4$ and its next two
steps are odd, even, with endpoint $w$.
If $3z+2=4w$, then $z\equiv2\pmod4$ and its next two
steps are even, odd, again with endpoint $w$.
Hence $X$ and $X+n$ reach $Y$ and $Y+m$ after two steps,
each having used one odd step. The induction hypothesis at parameter
$q+3t$ finishes the construction.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Synchronizing affine families}

To add an offset to a collection that already coalesces, we must
synchronize its current trajectory with the common trajectory of the
collection. Their coefficients in the remaining parameter can be
different powers of three.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:finite-affine-synchronization}
Let $A,B\in\mathbb Z_{>0}$ and $r,s\in\mathbb N$.
There exist $\ell,t_0,Q\in\mathbb N$ and
$C\in\mathbb Z_{>0}$ such that,
for every $v\in\mathbb N$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-affine-synchronization}
T^\ell\bigl(A+3^r(t_0+2^\ell v)\bigr)
=T^\ell\bigl(B+3^s(t_0+2^\ell v)\bigr)
=C+3^Qv.
\end{equation}
The two length-$\ell$ trajectories have fixed odd counts $u,w$
satisfying $r+u=s+w=Q$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Exchange the families if necessary so that $r\le s$.
Initially their current values satisfy
\[
Y=3^dX+D,\qquad d=s-r,\qquad D=B-3^dA\in\mathbb Z.
\]
At every subsequent stage the coefficient of the remaining parameter
in $X$ is a power of three and is therefore odd. We may prescribe its next
parity, as above. The displayed relation determines the parity of $Y$.
When $d>0$, the following steps reduce the relation to one with a
smaller exponent.

If $D\ne0$ is even, prescribe $X$ even. Both values take an even
step, and
\[
Y'=3^dX'+D/2.
\]
Repeating this finitely many times makes $D$ odd. This also applies
to negative $D$, by dividing out the power of two in $|D|$.
If $D=0$, prescribe $X$ odd. Both values take an odd step, and
\[
Y'=3^dX'+\frac{1-3^d}{2}.
\]
The new constant is nonzero because $d>0$, so the preceding
halving procedure makes it odd.
Once $D$ is odd, prescribe $X$ odd. Then $Y$ is even, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-ratio-reduction}
X'=\frac{3X+1}{2},\qquad Y'=\frac Y2,
\qquad
Y'=3^{d-1}X'+\frac{D-3^{d-1}}2.
\end{equation}
The last constant is an integer and the exponent has decreased.
There are finitely many steps at each fixed positive $d$ and finitely
many decreases of $d$, so eventually $d=0$.

The current values now have the same coefficient in the remaining
parameter and differ by a constant integer $D$. If $D=0$, they
are equal already. Otherwise, apply Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-pair}
with gap $|D|$ to the smaller current value. Suppose its required
progression is $a+2^kz$. The smaller value has the form
$E+3^qu$, so the congruence
\[
E+3^qu_0\equiv a\pmod{2^k}
\]
has a solution. Choose a nonnegative representative $u_0$ large
enough that $E+3^qu_0\ge a$. On the parameter class
$u=u_0+2^kv$, the smaller value belongs to the positive tail of
the required progression for every $v\ge0$.
The next $k$ steps therefore make the two values equal.

Each parameter restriction has modulus two for each added actual
step. Their composition has the form $t=t_0+2^\ell v$, where
$\ell$ is the common total number of steps. All bases and current
values remain positive. By Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-dyadic-prefix},
the resulting odd counts $u,w$ are independent of $v$, and the
two final affine coefficients are $3^{r+u}$ and $3^{s+w}$.
Equality at $v=0$ and $v=1$ forces these coefficients to agree.
This proves \eqref{eq:finite-affine-synchronization} and
$r+u=s+w=Q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:finite-pattern-coalescence}
For every finite $F\subset\mathbb N$, there are $K,R\in\mathbb N$
and $a,b\in\mathbb Z_{>0}$ such that, for every $n\in F$ and $t\in\mathbb N$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-pattern-coalescence}
T^K(a+2^Kt+n)=b+3^Rt,\qquad
o_K(a+2^Kt+n)=R.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Enlarge $F$ to include zero, and enumerate its offsets starting with
zero. For the initial singleton take $K=R=0$ and $a=b=1$.
Suppose the assertion holds for the offsets already included.
Their common current value on $a+2^Kt$ is $U(t)=b+3^Rt$.
For the next offset $n$, Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-dyadic-prefix}
gives
\[
T^K(a+2^Kt+n)=d+3^St,
\qquad d=T^K(a+n)>0,\quad S=o_K(a+n).
\]
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-affine-synchronization} to these two
affine families. On a class $t=t_0+2^\ell v$, they have the
same image after $\ell$ additional steps. Every previously included
trajectory starts those steps at exactly $U(t)$, so all previous
equalities are preserved.

The new starting progression is
\[
(a+2^Kt_0)+2^{K+\ell}v.
\]
Its modulus agrees with the new common time $K+\ell$.
If the two added trajectories use $u,w$ odd steps, the last
assertion of Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-affine-synchronization} gives
$R+u=S+w$. Thus all total odd counts agree as well.
The new endpoint has the required form $b'+3^{R+u}v$.
Finite induction proves the proposition.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Specializing the endpoint to a power of two}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:finite-power-two-residue}
Let $b\in\mathbb Z_{>0}$ with $3\nmid b$, and let $R\in\mathbb N$.
There are arbitrarily large $t\in\mathbb N$ for which
$b+3^Rt$ is a power of two.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We give the residue argument explicitly. For $m\ge1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-ternary-lift}
4^{3^{m-1}}\equiv1+3^m\pmod{3^{m+1}}.
\end{equation}
The case $m=1$ is immediate. Cubing a number of the form
$1+3^m(1+3c)$ proves the next case, since the terms beyond the
linear term are divisible by $3^{m+2}$.

Powers of two represent both units modulo three. Suppose
$2^s\equiv b\pmod{3^m}$. Multiplying $2^s$ by
$(4^{3^{m-1}})^j$, for $j=0,1,2$, changes its residue modulo
$3^{m+1}$ by $j2^s3^m$, by \eqref{eq:finite-ternary-lift}.
Since $3\nmid2^s$, these are the three possible lifts of its
residue modulo $3^m$. One therefore represents $b$ modulo
$3^{m+1}$. Induction gives $2^s\equiv b\pmod{3^R}$
for every $R\ge1$; the case $R=0$ is immediate.

The same identity gives
$2^{2\cdot3^R}\equiv1\pmod{3^R}$.
We may consequently increase $s$ by arbitrary multiples of
$2\cdot3^R$ while preserving the residue. For all sufficiently
large such exponents,
\[
t=\frac{2^s-b}{3^R}
\]
is a nonnegative integer, and these values of $t$ are unbounded.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-pattern-hitting}]
Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-pattern-coalescence} to
$F\cup\{0,1\}$, obtaining $K,R,a,b$.
We first show $3\nmid b$. An odd step has output congruent
to two modulo three. Thus a current value divisible by three has
only an even predecessor, which is also divisible by three.
If $3\mid b$, every step in any length-$K$ trajectory ending
at $b$ would be even, and its initial value would equal $2^Kb$.
The distinct starts $a$ and $a+1$ both end at $b$, a
contradiction.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-power-two-residue}, choose $t\ge1$
as large as necessary, with $b+3^Rt=2^s$, and put
$x=a+2^Kt$. The common endpoint after $K$ steps is $2^s$.
After $s$ further even steps, all trajectories reach one, with
total odd count $R$. Taking $t$ large ensures
$x>\max\{1,L\}$.

It remains to check that this is the first visit to one.
For $j<K$, Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-dyadic-prefix} gives
\[
T^j(x+n)=T^j(a+n)+2^{K-j}3^{o_j(a+n)}t>1.
\]
For $j=K+q<K+s$, the value is $2^{s-q}>1$.
Hence $H=K+s$ satisfies all of
\eqref{eq:finite-pattern-hitting}.
\end{proof}

For the unshortened map that sends odd $x$ to $3x+1$, each
of the $R$ odd steps above expands to an odd step followed by an
even step. The corresponding first hitting time is therefore
$H+R$, also common to the whole pattern. The inserted intermediate
values are greater than one, so first arrival is preserved.

The translated patterns in Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-pattern-hitting}
are selected by the construction. The theorem makes no assertion
that an arbitrary prescribed starting integer reaches one or enters
one of these patterns. In particular, it does not prove the Collatz
conjecture.
